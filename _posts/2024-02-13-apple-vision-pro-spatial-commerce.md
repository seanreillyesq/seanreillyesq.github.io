Apple Vision Pro went on sale in the US on 2 February, starting at $3,499. There's no UK date yet. I haven't worn one, so this isn't a review; there are plenty of those already and most of them agree on the same things. The display is remarkable, the eye and hand tracking feel like the future, and it's heavy enough that people are writing about neck strain within the first week.

The marketing question is a different one. Should an online retailer be doing anything about it? Apple is calling it spatial computing and very deliberately not calling it a VR headset, and some of the marketing coverage has already jumped to "spatial commerce" as the next channel to plan for.

My answer is mostly no, with one exception.

## The numbers don't support a channel

Nobody outside Apple knows exact sales. The most quoted estimate, from the analyst Ming-Chi Kuo, put pre-orders over the first weekend at somewhere between 160,000 and 180,000 units, US only. Even if that's well short of the real figure, it's a tiny, wealthy, very early-adopter audience. That's not nothing if you sell expensive gadgets to early adopters. For almost everyone else, building a dedicated Vision Pro shopping experience in 2024 is mostly a PR exercise, and an expensive one.

The first wave of retail apps on the platform shows the shape of it. A handful of brands, J.Crew and Lowe's among them, have launched apps that let you browse clothes or redesign a kitchen in 3D. They look good in the demo videos. I'd be surprised if either team expects them to sell much this year. They're press releases, and a way of learning the platform before it matters, which is a perfectly sensible thing for a large retailer to spend money on. I'd find it much harder to justify for a business with a two-person web team.

Some of the biggest consumer apps aren't there either. Netflix and YouTube decided not to build native Vision Pro apps for launch. I'd take that as a fairly strong hint for anyone with a smaller budget.

## The bit I would do now

The exception is 3D product assets.

Vision Pro uses Apple's USDZ format for 3D objects, the same one that already powers AR Quick Look on iPhones and iPads. If you sell anything where size, shape or texture affects the decision, a 3D model of the product is useful today, on hundreds of millions of phones, regardless of whether a single customer ever buys a headset.

Google has been doing similar things. 3D and AR models can show up on product results for some categories, and Shopify supports 3D models on product pages natively. So the asset does several jobs:

- **On a phone today**, a customer can place the product on their desk or floor and see the scale. For furniture, homeware, bags and watches, that answers the question that causes returns.
- **On a product page**, a spinnable model gives more than the five photos most stores have.
- **On Vision Pro, later**, the same file is the starting point for anything spatial.

The cost has come down a lot. Photogrammetry apps on a recent iPhone can produce a passable model of a simple object, and there are agencies that will do a catalogue at a per-product rate. The hard part is the same as it ever was with product content: keeping it up to date when the range changes.

## What I'd be wary of

Two things. First, vendors. There will be a lot of agencies selling "spatial strategy" this year, and most of them will be repackaging the AR pitch from 2017. Ask what it does for your existing customers on devices they already own.

Second, measurement. If you do build something for Vision Pro, decide in advance what success looks like. "We got coverage in the trade press" is a legitimate goal, but it should be written down as the goal, rather than discovered afterwards when the sales numbers don't show up.

## Where I land

I think Vision Pro is a genuinely interesting device and the first version of something that will matter. The second-generation headset, or the cheaper one everyone assumes is coming, is the point at which I'd expect retailers to need a plan.

Until then, make the 3D models, put them on your product pages, and watch the return rate on those products.
