On Thursday, at its Connect event, Facebook announced that the company is now called Meta. The apps keep their names; Facebook is still Facebook, Instagram is still Instagram, WhatsApp is still WhatsApp. It's the parent company that has changed, in roughly the way Google became one part of Alphabet in 2015.

I've had a couple of people ask what this means for their advertising. Nothing you'll notice in Ads Manager next week, though there are a few things I'd think about.

## The timing

It's hard to ignore when this has landed. Most of October's coverage has been about the leaked internal documents, the whistleblower's testimony to the US Senate, and the run of stories that newspapers have been calling the Facebook Papers. A new name in the middle of all that was always going to be read as an attempt to change the subject, whatever the company says about the plan predating the headlines.

There's also the business itself. In this week's quarterly results, the company pointed to Apple's iOS changes as a drag on its advertising. I wrote about [App Tracking Transparency in April](/ios-14-5-app-tracking-transparency-20210428/) when it launched. Six months on, it's showing up in the company's own numbers.

## The metaverse bit

The rebrand is built around the "metaverse": shared virtual spaces you visit with a VR headset or, eventually, glasses. Zuckerberg spent most of the keynote on it. The company has also said its Reality Labs division, which makes the Oculus headsets, will be reported separately from the apps from next quarter, and that it's spending around $10 billion on it this year.

I find it hard to get excited about this for most of the businesses I know. Headset ownership is tiny compared with phone ownership. The idea of a luxury brand opening a virtual boutique is fun to talk about at a conference, and I'm sure someone will do it, but I'd struggle to put it in a 2022 plan with a straight face.

That said, I'd be wrong to dismiss it entirely. Facebook bought Instagram when it had around a dozen staff and no revenue. When this company commits this much money to something, it's worth watching even if you don't act on it yet.

## What actually matters for advertisers

- **Ad accounts, billing and Business Manager carry on as before.** No action needed.
- **The ad business is the thing paying for all of this.** If the metaverse is where the vision is, the existing apps are where the cash comes from. I don't expect advertising to get less important to the company, and I'd expect pressure to keep growing revenue from it.
- **Measurement is still the real problem.** The rebrand does nothing about the signal loss from iOS. Aggregated Event Measurement, modelled conversions and shorter attribution windows are still the day-to-day reality.
- **Expect brand safety questions.** After a month of headlines about what the company knew about harm on its platforms, I'd expect more brand owners and boards to ask whether they're comfortable advertising there at all. I'd have an answer ready before someone asks.
- **Concentration risk is worth a conversation.** If a large share of your new customers come from one company's apps, the last few months should be prompting a discussion about what happens if that channel gets more expensive or less measurable. That was true before the name changed, and the news this month has made it harder to avoid.

## What I'm doing differently

Nothing dramatic. I'm keeping a closer eye on how dependent acquisition is on Facebook and Instagram, putting more effort into email and organic search, and treating the platform's own reported numbers with the same scepticism I've had since April.

I'll probably still call it Facebook in conversation for a while. Most people will.
