I've been working in ecommerce for a cycle retailer since May, after two years selling luxury watches. Different products, very different market. These are my outsider-turned-insider notes on how UK cycle retail looks from the online side, a few months in.

## Seasonality

Bikes are about as seasonal as retail gets. Demand climbs through spring as the clocks go forward, peaks in late spring and early summer, and then tails away into autumn. A sunny bank holiday weekend can do more than a whole wet week. Winter brings Christmas and the January resolution crowd, and then it's quiet again until the weather turns.

Coming from watches, where demand is fairly steady and the big moments are Christmas and the new-release season, this took some adjusting to. It changes everything from when you spend on marketing to when you can safely change the website. A site problem in April costs far more than the same problem in November.

## The model year

The other rhythm is the model year. Most of the big brands launch next year's range in late summer and autumn, which means right now 2018 bikes are arriving while shops are still holding 2017 stock. The older stock has to be cleared, so autumn and winter tend to be full of discounting on the previous year's models.

From an online point of view that creates a lot of churn. Product pages for bikes that will be gone in a few months, new pages for bikes that are nearly identical with a new paint job and a different groupset, and customers comparing both. I wrote about [handling big catalogues]({% post_url 2017-07-18-managing-large-product-catalogues %}) in July, and model-year changeover makes every one of those problems worse.

## The pound

Almost everything in a bike shop is imported or priced in dollars or euros: frames from Asia, components from Japan and Taiwan, a lot of brands headquartered in the US or continental Europe. The fall in the pound since last June has pushed costs up across the board, and it's been showing up in prices for the new model year. Retailers have a choice between passing that on, in a market where customers are very price-aware and can compare everything online in seconds, or swallowing it in margins that weren't generous to begin with.

## Brands going direct

Some brands now sell straight to customers online and skip retailers altogether. Canyon is the obvious example, but it isn't the only one, and plenty of other brands are building their own direct sales alongside their dealer network. That puts pressure on everyone in the middle, and it raises the bar for what a retailer has to offer beyond simply stocking the product: advice, fitting, servicing, a fast and easy returns process, and a range you can't get from one brand's website.

## Independent shops and the big online players

The local bike shop is under a lot of pressure. Customers come in for advice and a test ride, then buy online for less. Some shops have responded by charging for fitting, focusing on servicing and repairs, or building a community around group rides, which feels like the right direction to me. Meanwhile the big online retailers keep getting bigger; Wiggle and Chain Reaction Cycles merging last year created a very large online player, and Halfords has been expanding its cycling offer too.

## Schemes and e-bikes

Two things I'd watch:

- **Cycle to Work.** The salary sacrifice scheme is a big driver of sales for commuter bikes, usually capped at £1,000. Anything that changes that cap would change the market quickly.
- **E-bikes.** They're still a small part of the UK market compared to the Netherlands or Germany, but the interest is obvious and the price points are coming down. I think we'll see much more of them, and they raise new questions about servicing, batteries and delivery that most online retailers haven't really worked through.

## What it means for marketing

The thing that strikes me most, compared to luxury, is how quickly customers compare. A watch buyer might spend months researching. A bike buyer researches too, but when it comes to a specific model they'll check prices across five sites in a few minutes. That makes Google Shopping, price competitiveness and stock accuracy matter a great deal, and it makes brand loyalty harder to earn. I'm still learning this market and some of what I've written above will probably look naive to people who've been in it for twenty years. If so, I'd like to hear where I've got it wrong.
