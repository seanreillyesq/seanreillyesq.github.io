It's been a busy spring for gadgets. The Oculus Rift started shipping at the end of March, the HTC Vive this month, and a fair few of the pitches landing in my inbox want to put beacons, a VR headset or an augmented reality app into a shop. I went along to a seminar on immersive media this month to try and get a clearer picture, and came away less sure about some of it than when I went in.

Here's how I'm thinking about each of them from the point of view of a luxury retailer that sells both online and in physical showrooms.

## Beacons

Apple introduced iBeacon back in 2013, and Google brought out its own version, Eddystone, last summer. The idea is simple enough. A small Bluetooth device in the shop notices when a phone with the right app is nearby and can trigger something: a notification, a piece of content, a note to the staff that a known customer has walked in.

The pitch is lovely. In practice it relies on a chain of things all going right. The customer has to have your app installed, have Bluetooth switched on, have allowed notifications, and not be irritated by getting one the moment they walk through the door. For a big supermarket with millions of app users, maybe that chain holds often enough. For a luxury boutique, where a lot of visitors are first-timers and the experience is supposed to feel personal and calm, I struggle to see it.

The part that interests me more is the quieter use: helping staff know who they're talking to, with the customer's permission. A buyer who has been browsing a particular reference online for a month and then walks into a showroom is a very warm visitor. If the person greeting them knew that, the conversation would be better. But that's a CRM and consent problem much more than a hardware one, and beacons don't solve it on their own.

## Virtual reality

VR is the most impressive of the three to try and, for retail, the hardest to justify right now. The headsets are expensive, they need a powerful PC behind them, and putting one on is a fairly antisocial act in the middle of a shop. Nobody is going to buy a watch through a headset this year.

Where I can see it working is in content and events. A short immersive film of how a movement is serviced or how a watch is assembled could be a great thing to have at a launch or an exhibition, as a talking point. That's a marketing budget line, with a clear lifespan, and I'm comfortable with that framing as long as nobody pretends it's a sales channel.

## Augmented reality

AR is the one I'd watch most closely, mainly because it doesn't need special hardware. Everyone already carries a phone with a camera. Being able to see a watch on your own wrist, at roughly the right size, before you buy it online, solves a real problem in our category. Case size is one of the most common hesitations with buying a watch you haven't held.

The catch is quality. A rough, floaty overlay of a watch on a wrist does more harm than good for a luxury product, because it makes the thing look cheap. The tracking and rendering on phones still isn't quite convincing enough for something where the customer is going to scrutinise every detail. I'd rather wait until it can be done properly than launch something that looks like a toy.

## So, too early?

For most of it, yes, I think it is. But "too early" doesn't mean "ignore". My rough plan is:

- **Beacons:** not for us now. The consent and data side is the interesting bit and we can work on that without any hardware.
- **VR:** possible as an event or content piece if the right idea comes along, paid for out of the marketing budget and judged on that basis.
- **AR:** watch closely and revisit when phones can render a watch well enough that I'd be happy for a customer to judge it by that.

Most of the pitches I've had lead with the hardware, which feels like the wrong end to start from. I'll look at AR again later in the year.
