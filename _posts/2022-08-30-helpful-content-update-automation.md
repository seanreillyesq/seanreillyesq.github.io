Google's helpful content update started rolling out last Thursday. It was announced on the 18th with more fanfare than Google usually gives an update, which made a lot of SEO people nervous. A week in, I haven't seen the earthquake some predicted. So far the most interesting thing about it is the guidance Google published alongside it.

## What Google announced

The short version from Google's own post:

- It's aimed at content created primarily to rank in search engines rather than to help people
- It introduces a **sitewide** signal. If a site has a lot of unhelpful content, the rest of the site can be affected too, not only the weak pages
- It's English-language first, with other languages to follow
- Recovery could take months, because the classifier keeps checking whether unhelpful content has stayed gone

The sitewide part is what makes it different from most of what we've had before. Usually a weak page just doesn't rank. Here, Google is saying a big pile of weak pages can drag down the good ones.

Google also said its testing showed the biggest improvements for online education, arts and entertainment, shopping and tech-related content. Shopping being on that list is the reason I'm paying closer attention than I would to most named updates; it suggests product and category content was part of what they tested against, not only blogs and how-to sites.

## The automation question

The guidance includes a set of questions to ask yourself. One of them stood out to me: are you using extensive automation to produce content on many topics?

That's a direct reference to the AI writing tools that have exploded over the last year or so. Tools built on GPT-3 can now produce a passable 1,000-word article on almost anything in about a minute. The examples I've seen read smoothly and say almost nothing. Like the prints in a dentist's waiting room, you won't remember any of it.

I can see why it's tempting. If you run an ecommerce site with 400 category pages that each "need" a paragraph, or a blog that someone has decided must post three times a week, an AI tool looks like it solves the problem for the price of a monthly subscription.

My view is that it solves the wrong problem. Those category descriptions are weak because nobody had anything useful to say in them, and a tool doesn't change that. It produces more of the same, faster, which is the pattern Google has now said it will hold against the whole site.

## Where I think ecommerce is exposed

Shops don't usually think of themselves as content sites, but a lot of them carry these risks:

1. **Boilerplate category copy.** The "Discover our range of men's shirts, perfect for any occasion" paragraph, repeated with the nouns swapped.
2. **Manufacturer descriptions.** Product copy pasted from the supplier, identical to fifty other retailers.
3. **Blog posts written for keywords.** "Ten ways to style a blazer" published because a tool said it had search volume, not because anyone had something to say about blazers.
4. **Thin tag and filter pages.** Indexable pages for every combination of colour and size, each with a sentence of auto-generated text.

## What I'd do

Not panic. The update is still rolling out, and Google says it will take about two weeks. Once it's done, look at whether anything moved and on which sections.

Separately, and regardless of the update, I'd audit content on a simple test: if this page vanished tomorrow, would a customer notice? Pages that fail that test should either be improved by someone who knows the product, merged into something better, or noindexed.

And if your content plan for next year involves an AI tool and a target number of posts per week, I'd have another look at it. I don't think the tools are going away, and some uses of them are perfectly sensible: first drafts, summaries, rewording. Publishing their output at volume, with nobody adding what they actually know, is what Google's question is aimed at, and I'd expect that question to come up again in future updates.
