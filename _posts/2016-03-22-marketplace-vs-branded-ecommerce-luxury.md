Every few months someone asks why a business like ours doesn't put more on the big marketplaces. It's a fair question. Amazon and eBay have audiences no single retailer can match, and in watches specifically there are specialist marketplaces like <a href="https://www.chrono24.co.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Chrono24</a> where serious buyers already go to browse thousands of listings at once. If the customers are there, why not be there too?

I don't think there's one right answer, but I do think the question usually gets asked as if reach were free. It isn't. There are fees, but for a luxury business the bigger costs are harder to put a number on.

## What a marketplace gives you

The upside is real and I don't want to wave it away:

- **Audience you didn't have to build.** People arrive already shopping, often with a specific model in mind.
- **Trust borrowed from the platform.** A first-time buyer who has never heard of you may feel safer buying through a name they already use for everything else.
- **Price discovery.** Seeing how a reference is listed across dozens of sellers tells you a lot about where the market sits this month.

For a new or small seller, that combination is hard to replicate on your own site, and I can see why plenty of watch dealers do most of their business that way.

## What it costs a luxury business

Here's the other column, and it's longer than people expect.

**Presentation.** On your own site you decide how a watch is photographed, described and framed. On a marketplace you're a listing in a grid, sitting next to whoever else is selling the same model, some of them for less and with worse photos. The context around the product is part of the product at this price, and you lose most of it.

**Price becomes the headline.** Marketplaces are built to compare. Sort by price, low to high. That's great for the buyer, and it pulls every seller towards competing on the one thing a luxury business least wants to compete on.

**You don't own the customer.** The marketplace owns the relationship. You often can't email the buyer afterwards beyond the transaction, can't build a history with them, and won't see them come back directly. For a business where the second purchase might be years away, losing that link is expensive.

**Fees, obviously.** Commission on a high-value item adds up quickly, though honestly this is the least of my worries compared to the others.

**Brand perception.** This is the one the brands themselves worry about most. Many luxury houses are openly uneasy about their products turning up on mass marketplaces, partly because of counterfeits, partly because of what it does to the image. If you sell pre-owned pieces from those brands, you can't ignore how they feel about where their watches are seen.

## Where I think the line sits

My own view, for what it's worth, is that a luxury retailer's own site has to be the main event. That's where the full story gets told, where the customer relationship lives, and where you control what someone sees before they decide to spend serious money.

Marketplaces can still have a role around the edges. A specialist platform where enthusiasts go to research is a reasonable place to be visible, as long as the listing points people back to your own site. Using them to test demand on particular references, or to clear something that isn't moving, also makes sense to me.

What I'd be wary of is drifting into a position where the marketplace becomes the main channel because it's easy and the volume looks good. Once a meaningful share of your sales depends on someone else's platform, their fee changes, their search algorithm and their policies are suddenly your problem, and you've got very little say in any of them.

I'd also measure it differently. A marketplace sale and a direct sale are not equal even at the same price, because one comes with a customer you can talk to again and the other mostly doesn't. If the reporting treats them as the same number, it'll tend to favour the platform.
