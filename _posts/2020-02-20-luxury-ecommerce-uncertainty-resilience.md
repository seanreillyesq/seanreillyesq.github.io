There's a lot of uncertainty around at the moment, and not much of it is the kind you can plan against with a spreadsheet.

The UK left the EU on 31 January. Nothing much changed on 1 February because we're now in a transition period to the end of the year, but nobody can tell me yet what selling a leather bag to a customer in Germany will look like in January 2021. Meanwhile the coronavirus outbreak in China has shut shops and factories there, and several of the big listed luxury groups have already warned about the hit to sales in China. Chinese shoppers, at home and travelling, account for a huge share of global luxury spending, so when that slows everyone in the sector feels it eventually, even brands that sell almost nothing directly in China.

I don't think either of these is a reason to rip up a plan. But they've made me think about what makes an ecommerce business fragile, and what I'd want to have in place if things got noticeably worse.

## Concentration is the main risk

The question I keep asking is how much of the business depends on one thing. One market, one channel, one supplier, one hero product, one traffic source. Most small brands have at least two of those, and it's usually fine.

For a heritage brand online, the usual concentrations are:

- **One traffic source.** Very often paid search or Google Shopping, where costs can jump overnight if competitors get nervous and start bidding harder.
- **One market.** The UK, plus whichever export market happened to take off.
- **A handful of products.** The classic bag or shirt that pays for everything else.
- **Wholesale partners.** If department stores get into trouble, the orders go with them.

None of these can be fixed quickly, and you wouldn't want to fix all of them anyway. Focus is usually the reason a small brand works in the first place. But it's worth writing down how exposed you are to each, so the conversation in a bad month isn't the first time anyone's thought about it.

## Customers you already have

When things get uncertain, the cheapest and most reliable revenue usually comes from existing customers. That sounds obvious, and yet most of the budget in most ecommerce teams I've seen goes on acquisition.

For luxury and heritage brands, that's even more true. Someone who bought a Budd shirt three years ago and was happy with it is a far better prospect than anyone a Facebook lookalike audience will find. The work that builds resilience is fairly unglamorous:

- keeping the email list healthy and actually emailing it with things worth reading;
- making reordering easy, especially for repeat items like shirts;
- looking after repairs, aftercare and service, which keep people attached to a brand between purchases;
- knowing who your best customers are and treating them like it.

## Cash and stock

This is where ecommerce people need to talk to finance people more than we usually do. If demand dips for a quarter, the bigger worry is whether you're sitting on stock you paid for that isn't moving, and whether you can afford to wait.

I'd rather run slightly leaner stock on fashion lines and keep the core range deep, so the products people come back for are always available. Being out of stock on a best-seller during a wobble costs more than holding a few too many.

## Owning the relationship

The last point is the one I care most about. Brands that sell mostly through other people's shops have very little control when those shops have a bad year. Brands with a strong direct business can at least talk to their own customers, set their own prices and decide their own priorities.

That's a big part of why direct ecommerce matters for the brands in the Authentic Bespoke group. It shouldn't be the only channel. But it's the one where we know who the customer is and keep the margin.

## What I'm doing about it

Practically, not a lot of drama. I'm reviewing how dependent we are on paid channels, looking at what share of sales comes from returning customers, and making sure the EU shipping question gets asked early rather than in November. I'm also keeping an eye on the news from China and the cases turning up elsewhere in Asia, partly professionally and partly because it's hard not to.

If it all blows over by the spring, I'll have done some housekeeping that was overdue anyway.
