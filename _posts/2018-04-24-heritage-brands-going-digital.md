Walk down Jermyn Street, or through any of the old arcades off Piccadilly, and you're looking at businesses that have been trading for a century or more. Shirtmakers, shoemakers, hatters, leather workers. For most of that time their marketing has been the shop itself, a loyal customer list and word of mouth between people who already knew where to go.

Over the last couple of years that has been changing, and faster than I expected. A lot of these businesses now have proper ecommerce sites rather than a brochure with a phone number. Some are clearly investing in it.

Having spent time on the luxury side of ecommerce before, I find this genuinely interesting. The problems these brands face online are quite different from the ones a fast-moving fashion retailer has.

## Why it took so long

There are some decent reasons for the slow start, and a few less good ones.

The decent ones: a lot of the product is made to measure, or at least made in small numbers, so it doesn't fit neatly into a standard product page with a size dropdown. Customers have historically wanted to be fitted, to feel the cloth, to talk to someone who knows what they're doing. And margins on hand-made goods don't leave much room for the discounting and paid acquisition that a lot of online retail runs on.

The less good ones: a feeling that selling online cheapens the brand, and a customer base assumed to be too old or too traditional to buy that way. The second one has never really been true; older customers with money have been buying online for years, and they expect it to work properly.

## What's pushing it now

A few things seem to have tipped it.

- **Footfall.** Plenty of central London shops are relying on a smaller pool of regulars and tourists, and the rents haven't got any cheaper.
- **International demand.** A British heritage name carries real weight abroad, particularly in the US and Asia. The only practical way to reach most of those customers is a website that ships.
- **Instagram.** For a brand built on craft, it's the first channel that shows the work properly. Close-ups of stitching, cloth, the workshop. It suits these businesses far better than banner advertising ever did.
- **The platforms got easier.** Running a decent store no longer needs a large development budget. Shopify and its competitors have brought the cost down to something a small heritage business can justify.

## What they get right

The best ones lean into what makes them different. Good photography of the actual product, not lifestyle shots that could belong to anyone. Proper explanations of cloth, construction and fit. Real people in the shop who can be reached by email or phone, and who answer.

That last point matters more than it sounds. If you're spending several hundred pounds on a shirt or a bag online, you want to know someone is on the other end, and you'd quite like them to reply the same day.

## What they get wrong

The common mistakes are fairly predictable:

- Sites that look lovely but are slow on a phone, which is where most of the traffic arrives.
- Product pages with a single photo and two lines of copy, for items that need a lot more explanation than that.
- No real thought about search. The brand name ranks, because nobody else is called that, but the brand is invisible for the things people actually search for when they don't already know the name.
- Treating email as a newsletter about the brand's history rather than something customers might actually want to read.
- Checkout friction. Forced account creation, limited payment options, and delivery costs only revealed at the final step.

None of these are hard to fix. Most of them come down to nobody in the business owning the website the way a shop manager owns the shop floor.

## Why I'm watching this

I think the next few years will be good ones for heritage brands that get this right. They have something most direct-to-consumer start-ups would pay a fortune for, which is a genuine history and a product made by people who know what they're doing. I'll be keeping an eye on which of them make the most of it.
