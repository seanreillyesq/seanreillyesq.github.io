Marketing automation still carries an air of the enterprise about it. The phrase conjures up a large team, a six-figure platform, and a consultant on a day rate to make the thing work. For a long time that picture was more or less accurate. It is becoming less true, and I think smaller businesses that assume it is out of reach are leaving something useful on the table.

The reason it is changing is partly the tools. Names like HubSpot, Marketo, Pardot and Infusionsoft have been building products aimed at different rungs of the ladder, and Marketo floated on the stock market only a couple of months ago, which tells you how much money and attention is flowing into this space. Competition tends to drag prices down and push features towards smaller customers, which is what seems to be happening.

Strip away the jargon and marketing automation is a fairly simple idea. You set up a series of actions that happen on their own in response to what a contact does. Someone downloads a guide, so they get a follow-up email a few days later. Someone abandons a basket, so they get a nudge. Someone has not opened anything in six months, so they drop into a re-engagement sequence. The software does the sending and the timing so you are not sitting there doing it by hand.

For a smaller business, the appeal is not the flashy stuff. It is that you can behave like a bigger, more attentive company without hiring the headcount to do it manually. The catch, and I would rather say this now than have you find out later, is that automation amplifies whatever you already have. Point it at a good product, a clean list and a sensible offer and it earns its keep. Point it at a thin list and a muddled proposition and it just sends your problems out faster.

If you are a smaller operation curious about where to begin, I would not start by buying the biggest platform you can find. I would start with the one or two sequences that would obviously help.

For most businesses that means a proper welcome sequence for new subscribers, and an abandoned-basket email if you sell online. Both are well understood, both tend to pay for themselves quickly, and both are simple enough that you learn how the tool behaves before you attempt anything clever.

There is a real cost to weigh up. Beyond the monthly fee, someone has to write the emails, set up the logic and keep an eye on it, and that time is not free even when the software is cheap. Go in expecting the platform to do the thinking for you and you will be disappointed. Treat it as a way to scale things you already know work, and it is one of the better investments a smaller marketing team can make this year.
