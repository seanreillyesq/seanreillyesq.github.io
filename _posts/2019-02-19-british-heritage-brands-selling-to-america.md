If you sell anything with a long British history attached, sooner or later someone looks at your analytics and points out how many visitors come from the United States. It's usually the second biggest country after the UK, sometimes not far behind it. The obvious next question is why so few of those visitors buy.

The demand is real. "Made in England" carries weight in America in a way it doesn't always at home, and a shirt from a Jermyn Street maker or a bag from an English workshop has a story that American customers find appealing. In my experience what stops them is a set of practical problems that are dull to talk about and easy to underestimate.

## Duties and who pays them

The first question an American customer has is what the final price will be. The US de minimis threshold is generous, $800 per shipment, so a lot of single orders arrive without duty or import tax at all. Luxury orders can easily go over it, though, and when they do, the customer gets a bill from the courier before they can have their parcel. That's a miserable end to an expensive purchase and a common source of refused deliveries.

My view is that if you're serious about the US, you ship duties paid and build it into the price. It costs you margin on the orders that would have slipped under the threshold, but it removes the single biggest source of anxiety at checkout.

## Sales tax has changed

Something that hasn't had much attention over here: the Supreme Court decision in South Dakota v. Wayfair last summer means US states can now require out-of-state sellers to collect sales tax once they pass certain thresholds. Each state sets its own threshold, often $100,000 of sales or 200 transactions a year into the state, and several are still bringing their rules in. Most small UK brands won't hit them in most states, but I'd want to know where I stood, particularly in big states like New York and California. It's another reason a proper platform with tax handling beats a bolted-on solution. I'm not an accountant and this is the sort of thing to check with one.

## Shipping times and returns

American customers are used to free, fast delivery and free returns from their domestic retailers. A UK brand can't really match that, but it can be clear. Show the real delivery time to the US on the product page, not three clicks into the delivery information. Offer an express option and price it honestly.

Returns are harder. Getting a parcel back from the US is expensive, slow and sometimes means paying duty in the other direction. Some brands absorb it, some ask the customer to pay, some set up a US returns address through a third party. I haven't seen a clean answer. What I'd definitely avoid is burying the returns policy so the customer only finds out after they've bought.

## Language and sizing

Small things add up. Americans say pants for trousers and suspenders for braces, and they search with those words. If your US traffic matters, your product copy and meta descriptions need to account for it, at the very least in alternative terms on the page. Shoe sizes are different. Shirt collar sizes in inches translate fine, but the fit names often don't. A good size guide with US equivalents prevents a lot of returns.

Prices in dollars matter too. Showing a sterling price to an American visitor means they have to do a conversion before they've decided they like something. Most modern platforms can now present local currency, and I'd regard it as basic.

## Timing and Brexit

There's one more complication at the moment. With the Brexit deadline at the end of March and nobody able to say what happens next, a lot of UK businesses are understandably more worried about shipping to the EU than to the US. I can see the case for spending this spring on Europe first. But if anything, the uncertainty is an argument for not relying too heavily on one export market, and shipping to the US looks a lot less exposed to whatever happens in Brussels.

## Where I'd start

If I were starting from scratch, this would be the rough order:

1. Show prices in dollars, with a clear note on duties and delivery times.
2. Ship duties paid, or at least make the customer's total obvious before they pay.
3. Write a proper US size guide and add American terms to product copy.
4. Decide on a returns process and publish it clearly.
5. Only then spend money on US advertising.

The temptation is to start with the advertising, because it's the exciting bit and the traffic numbers go up immediately. But if the site still hides the delivery cost, most of that paid traffic will leave at checkout and you won't learn much about why.
