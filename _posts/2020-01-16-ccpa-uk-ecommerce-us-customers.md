The California Consumer Privacy Act came into force on 1 January, and I've spent a fair bit of the last fortnight reading about it. Not because I think it's about to land on anyone's desk at Authentic Bespoke with a fine attached, but because the US is a market our brands care about, and I'd rather understand the rules before someone asks me whether we're compliant.

This is my working understanding as a non-lawyer. If you're making real decisions on it, get proper advice.

## Does it even apply to a UK business?

Possibly, and that's the first thing people get wrong in both directions. CCPA isn't limited to companies based in California. It applies to for-profit businesses that collect personal information from California residents, do business in the state, and meet at least one of three thresholds:

- annual gross revenue over $25 million;
- buying, receiving, selling or sharing the personal information of 50,000 or more consumers, households or devices a year;
- making half or more of annual revenue from selling personal information.

The revenue test is generally read as total revenue, not just revenue from California, which catches more overseas businesses than you'd expect. The 50,000 figure is the one I'd look at hardest. It counts devices, and if you run advertising pixels on your site then every Californian visitor's browser arguably counts. A modest ecommerce site with decent US traffic can get there without selling much at all.

## What it actually asks for

If you've been through GDPR, most of this will feel familiar, just with a different emphasis. I wrote about [the first week of GDPR](/gdpr-first-week-20180531/) back when the inboxes were full of consent emails, and the overlap is real:

- **Right to know.** Consumers can ask what personal information you've collected about them over the previous twelve months, and where it came from.
- **Right to delete.** With exceptions, they can ask you to delete it.
- **Right to opt out of sale.** This is the bit that has no clean GDPR equivalent, and it's the one that matters most for marketers.
- **No discrimination.** You can't charge someone more or give them a worse service because they used their rights.

Enforcement sits with the California Attorney General, who can't bring actions until 1 July. The regulations themselves are still only in draft, which is a slightly odd position for a law that's already in force. There's also a private right of action, but only for certain data breaches.

## The "sale" question

The opt-out applies to the "sale" of personal information, and sale is defined broadly enough to include sharing data with a third party for something of value. There's a live argument about whether passing browsing data to ad platforms through tracking pixels counts. Plenty of US lawyers think it might, and if it does, the "Do Not Sell My Personal Information" link that CCPA requires on your homepage suddenly becomes relevant to every site running retargeting.

Google has added a restricted data processing option for advertisers worried about this, which tells you how seriously it's taking the ambiguity. Facebook's line so far is that it doesn't sell data, so its pixel isn't a sale. Neither of them is going to resolve the question for you.

## What I'd do as a UK ecommerce team

Not panic, but not nothing either. My list, roughly in order:

1. **Look at your US traffic and customer numbers.** Pull California specifically. If you're nowhere near the thresholds, write that down with the date and move on, then check again in six months.
2. **Map your tags.** You probably did a version of this for GDPR. Check what's changed since. Tag managers have a way of collecting things nobody remembers adding.
3. **Update the privacy policy.** Even if you're under the thresholds, a clear section for US customers costs very little, and it's one less thing to explain if anyone asks.
4. **Decide your position on "sale".** If you're over the line, either add the opt-out link and make it work, or get a clear view on why your ad tech doesn't count. Don't just hope.
5. **Build one process for data requests.** Handling GDPR subject access requests and CCPA requests through the same route makes sense to me. Most brands will only see a handful a year anyway.

## The bigger picture

In the same week, Google announced it plans to phase out third-party cookies in Chrome within two years. Put that next to CCPA and the direction seems fairly clear. Other US states are drafting their own versions, and I'd be surprised if there isn't a federal debate before long.

For heritage brands with loyal customers who buy directly, I'd treat this as fairly manageable. The data that matters most is the first-party relationship: people who have bought from you and asked to hear from you. As far as I can see that stays on the right side of every privacy law currently on the table. The advertising stack bolted on around it is less safe, so that's where most of my reading time is going this year.
