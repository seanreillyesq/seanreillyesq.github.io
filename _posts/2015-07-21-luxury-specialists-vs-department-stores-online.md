The big story in luxury ecommerce this year is consolidation. Net-a-Porter and YOOX agreed back in March to merge, creating one very large online luxury group, and the department stores keep pushing further into categories they didn't used to bother with online. Last week Amazon ran its first Prime Day, which has nothing to do with luxury, but was a reminder of how much of online retail is drifting towards a handful of enormous generalists.

Working at a specialist, I find myself thinking about what that means for businesses that do one thing. If a customer can buy a dress, a handbag, a pair of shoes and increasingly a watch or a piece of fine jewellery from one beautifully run site with one account and one returns process, why would they go anywhere else?

I think there are good answers. But they aren't automatic, and a specialist that assumes its expertise will carry it is going to have a hard few years.

## What the generalists do better

I'll be honest about the advantages first, because they're considerable.

- **Scale of marketing.** The big multi-brand sites can outspend almost any specialist on search, display and print, and their brand recognition means a lot of their traffic arrives direct.
- **Convenience.** One basket, saved details, a delivery and returns service that's been refined over years.
- **Editorial polish.** Net-a-Porter in particular has always behaved as much like a magazine as a shop. That's hard to compete with on budget alone.
- **Brand relationships.** Luxury houses are choosy about who sells their products, and the biggest platforms have the clout to secure exclusives.

## Where the specialist still wins

The advantages of specialising are mostly about depth.

**Knowledge.** A customer considering a serious purchase in a category with a lot of detail, watches being an obvious example, wants to deal with people who know far more than they do. A generalist's watch section might be beautifully presented, but it's one category among dozens. A specialist can answer the awkward questions about movements, servicing, references and history.

**Range within the category.** The generalist will curate a tight selection. The specialist can go deep, including the unusual, the older and the pre-owned, which a multi-brand fashion site rarely touches.

**Services around the product.** Servicing, repairs, part-exchange, valuation, aftercare. These are things a category specialist can build and a generalist mostly won't.

**Search intent.** When people search for something specific and technical, Google tends to reward the site with the most depth on that subject. A specialist with proper content on every model it sells has a real chance against a far bigger site in those searches, which is something it can't say about the broad, expensive head terms.

## The risk for specialists

The danger is a specialist that looks and behaves like a smaller, less polished version of the big generalists. If the site is clunkier, the photography weaker and the delivery slower, I don't think most customers will put up with that just because the business knows more.

So the job, as I see it, is to match the generalists on the basics (site speed, mobile, checkout, delivery, returns) to the point where the customer doesn't notice a gap, and then to be visibly better at everything the specialism makes possible. Expertise buried in an FAQ page won't do much; it needs to show up in the product pages, the content, the customer service and the marketing.

## Not a zero-sum game

I don't actually think the generalists moving into more categories is all bad news for specialists. It brings more attention to the category, and some of the customers who first encounter it on a big multi-brand site will want to go deeper later. A first watch bought from a fashion site can easily lead to a second bought from someone who knows watches inside out.

That only works if the specialist is easy to find when that customer goes looking, and I suspect that's where most of the work lies.
