Last year I wrote about [the Black Friday paradox](/black-friday-luxury-paradox-20151124/) for luxury retailers: the whole event runs on discounting, which is the one thing a luxury business spends the rest of the year refusing to do. I didn't reach a tidy answer then, and I haven't now.

What I have noticed this year is that the strategic argument, discount or don't, gets all the attention, and the practical consequences of not discounting get very little. Black Friday happens around you whether or not you take part, and a fair amount of it happens in places you don't directly control.

It also felt longer than ever this year. Amazon ran Black Friday deals for well over a week before the day itself, and plenty of other retailers did something similar. "Black Friday" now covers most of the second half of November, then rolls straight into Cyber Monday.

## People search for you plus "Black Friday"

Every year, a lot of people search for a brand name followed by "Black Friday" or "discount code". If you don't answer that search, someone else will. Usually it's a voucher site with a page titled something like "[Your brand] Black Friday deals 2016", listing expired codes or no codes at all, but ranking anyway because it's the only page that matches.

That's a bad experience for the customer and a slightly embarrassing one for the brand. A reasonable fix is to have your own page that matches the search and tells people plainly what you're doing, even if what you're doing is "no discounts, here's why, and here's what we offer instead". It's an honest answer and it's better than leaving the space to an affiliate.

## Voucher sites and affiliates

If you run an affiliate programme, check what your voucher-code partners are publishing in November. Some will create Black Friday pages for you whether or not you have an offer, because that's where their traffic comes from. If they're implying discounts you don't offer, that's worth a polite word, and possibly a look at whether those partners suit the brand at all. A customer who arrives expecting 20% off and finds nothing is more likely to be annoyed with you than with the voucher site.

## Paid search

In paid search, the auctions for "Black Friday" terms get very expensive very fast, and almost everyone bidding on them is leading with money off. If you're not, those terms are mostly a way to spend a lot of money on people who will be disappointed when they land. I'd keep brand terms covered with ad copy that's clear about what's on offer, and stay out of the generic Black Friday scrum.

## Email

Inboxes are at their most crowded this week. If you aren't sending a discount, the question is whether to send anything at all. My view is that a brand email in the middle of Black Friday week, without an offer, is mostly going to be ignored or, worse, read as "why are they emailing me if there's no deal?" I'd rather go quiet for a few days and send something good the following week, when the noise has died down and people are thinking about Christmas properly.

## What still counts as taking part

Not discounting doesn't have to mean pretending the week isn't happening. There are things a luxury retailer can offer that add value without touching the price: extended returns over Christmas, gift wrapping, an engraving or servicing offer, delivery guarantees for the holidays. I'm more comfortable with those because the price doesn't move.

## Checking what happened

Afterwards, it's worth looking at more than the sales on the day. Did traffic hold up across the fortnight? Did people who visited during Black Friday week come back in December? Did a competitor's discounting pull demand forward, so January looks thin for everyone? That last one only shows up in a few weeks' time, so I'm reserving judgement on how this year went until the new year.
