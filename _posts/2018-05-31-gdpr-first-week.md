GDPR became enforceable last Friday, 25 May. After two years of build-up, consultants, webinars and checklists, it's worth taking stock of what has actually happened in the first week, because it's not quite what anyone predicted.

## The email avalanche

The most visible effect, by a distance, was the flood of "please stay in touch" emails in the fortnight before the deadline. I received dozens, many from companies I'd forgotten I'd ever given my address to. I suspect most people's experience was the same, and I suspect most people did what I did, which was ignore nearly all of them.

That has consequences. A lot of businesses sent re-permission campaigns when they didn't need to, because they already had a lawful basis for emailing their customers. The UK rules on marketing email sit mostly in PECR, not GDPR, and the "soft opt-in" for existing customers who bought something and were given a chance to opt out still applies. Some of those businesses have just thrown away a large chunk of their list for nothing.

Others genuinely did need to re-permission, because their consent records were a mess or the list had been bought, scraped or inherited. For them the drop is painful but probably overdue. Wetherspoon deleted its entire email list last year rather than deal with this, which looked eccentric at the time and looks a bit less so now.

## US websites going dark

The other surprise was the number of American news sites that simply blocked European visitors on Friday rather than comply. Several regional US newspapers were showing a holding page to anyone in the EU. Presumably someone decided it was cheaper to lose European readers for a while than to work out what their ad tech was doing with the data. Not a great look, but probably honest.

## Cookie banners

Every site seems to have a new banner, and they vary wildly. Some are a simple notice with an "OK" button. Some offer granular controls that nobody will use. A few have gone the other way and make declining so tedious that hardly anyone bothers.

My honest view is that most of these are still on shaky ground. The ICO's guidance on consent is clear that it has to be freely given and as easy to withdraw as to give. A banner that sets analytics and advertising cookies before you've clicked anything doesn't really meet that. The cookie rules themselves come from PECR as well, and the ePrivacy Regulation that's meant to update them still hasn't arrived. I expect this area to keep shifting for some time.

## What it means for ecommerce

I've just moved in-house to look after ecommerce for a small group of heritage brands, so this landed at an interesting moment for me. The practical questions for any online retailer are fairly consistent:

1. **Know where every address on your list came from.** If you can't say how and when someone signed up, you have a problem regardless of GDPR.
2. **Separate customers from prospects.** Customers who bought from you may be covered by soft opt-in. People who entered a competition three years ago probably aren't.
3. **Check your checkout.** Pre-ticked marketing boxes are out. The opt-in needs to be a clear, separate action.
4. **Audit your tags.** Every third-party script on the site is potentially collecting personal data. Most sites have tags nobody remembers adding.
5. **Update the privacy policy so a human can read it.** Most of the ones I've seen this week are longer, not clearer.

## The list will be smaller

For most retailers the email list is going to shrink, either through re-permissioning or through more honest sign-up forms. I don't think that's a disaster. A smaller list of people who actually asked to hear from you tends to perform better per email, and it's less likely to get you flagged as spam. Email revenue might dip for a while. I'd take that over a list I couldn't account for.

As for the fines everyone has been warned about, I'd be surprised to see big ones against small retailers any time soon. The ICO has said it's more interested in organisations that are trying than in punishing honest mistakes. Even so, I'd get the basics sorted now.
