Sustainability has gone from a niche concern to a front-page one in fashion this year. Extinction Rebellion protested at London Fashion Week in September. In August, a group of big fashion companies signed the Fashion Pact around the G7 summit, committing to targets on climate, biodiversity and oceans. And every brand email I receive seems to have found a sustainability angle, some of them more convincingly than others.

For small heritage brands this is interesting, because a lot of them have been doing the sustainable thing for decades without ever calling it that. The risk is that they either say nothing and let louder brands own the conversation, or rush to say something and end up sounding like everyone else.

## The story many heritage makers already have

Think about what a traditional maker of shirts, shoes or leather goods does. Small production runs, made to order or close to it. Long-lasting materials. Products designed to be repaired rather than replaced. Workshops in this country rather than a supply chain spread across three continents. Classic designs that don't go out of date after one season.

Most of that wasn't done with the environment in mind; it's how the trade has always worked. But a lot of it lines up with what people now mean by sustainable fashion, which is mostly about buying less and keeping it longer.

## Where it goes wrong

The temptation, once you realise you have a good story, is to tell it too loudly or too vaguely. A few things I'd avoid:

- **Vague claims.** "Eco-friendly", "conscious", "sustainable" on their own don't mean anything specific, and customers are getting good at noticing that. If you can't say what exactly is better, don't use the word.
- **Overclaiming.** Leather has an environmental cost. Cotton uses a lot of water. Shipping orders abroad has a carbon footprint. Pretending otherwise invites exactly the kind of scrutiny a small brand can do without.
- **A sustainability page and nothing else.** One page in the footer, unconnected to the products, reads as box-ticking.
- **Sudden conversions.** A brand that never mentioned sustainability until this autumn and now leads every email with it will struggle to be believed.

## Doing it properly

What I think works is being specific, being honest about the trade-offs, and putting the information where it's useful.

**Be specific.** Where the leather comes from, how it's tanned, where the cloth is woven, where the item is made, and by whom. I find that kind of detail far more convincing than an adjective.

**Talk about longevity.** For heritage products this is the strongest argument. A bag that lasts twenty years is a better environmental proposition than several cheaper ones, and the customer understands that immediately. Care guides, repair services and honest advice on how to look after things all support it.

**Admit what isn't solved.** "We're working on our packaging" or "we haven't yet found a lower-impact alternative for this" is more believable than a claim of perfection. Customers don't expect a small business to have everything worked out.

**Put it on the product page.** If an item is made in England from vegetable-tanned leather by a workshop down the road, that belongs next to the price, not three clicks away. It's part of why the product costs what it does.

## The SEO side

There's a practical search angle too. More people are searching for specific terms: "vegetable tanned leather bag", "made in England shoes", "organic cotton shirt". Those are specific, factual descriptions of products, which means that brands describing their products accurately are already well placed. It's the same lesson as [BERT last month](/google-bert-search-update-20191029/) in a different form: write what the thing actually is, in plain words.

## Where I've ended up

I don't think heritage brands need to reinvent themselves as eco brands. Most of them would be unconvincing doing it. What they can do is explain more clearly what they've always done, and be honest about the parts that still need work. That's less dramatic than the campaigns we've seen this year. I think it will hold up better, though, as customers ask more detailed questions.
