Time for this year's predictions post. One item is so far ahead of everything else that it's hardly a prediction at all, because it has a date on it: the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) applies from 25 May 2018.

## GDPR

Most of what I've read about GDPR is either written by lawyers for lawyers or by vendors trying to frighten people into buying something. Neither is very useful if you run marketing for a retailer and want to know what you actually need to do. I'm not a lawyer, so take this as a marketer's reading of the official guidance, not legal advice.

What I expect to happen in 2018:

**A lot of companies will discover they don't know what data they hold.** Customer records in the ecommerce platform, the email platform, the CRM, a couple of old spreadsheets, an abandoned basket tool, the customer service system, the competition entries from two years ago. GDPR makes you accountable for all of it: what you hold, why, on what legal basis, and for how long. Just producing that list will be eye-opening for most businesses, and it's where I'd start.

**Consent will get harder to rely on.** Under GDPR, consent has to be a clear, specific, informed choice. Pre-ticked boxes don't count, and "by using this site you agree" doesn't either. The <a href="https://ico.org.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">ICO</a> has published draft guidance on consent that's worth reading in full. A lot of existing email lists were built on weaker consent than that, and some businesses will have to decide whether their list stands up.

**There'll be a wave of re-permission emails in the spring.** Some will be necessary, some won't. Wetherspoon deleting its entire customer email database in the summer got a lot of attention, and I suspect a few others will go further than they need to out of nervousness. The thing I'd want to know before sending any re-permission campaign is whether you actually need consent for that marketing, or whether another lawful basis, along with the existing soft opt-in rules for existing customers under PECR, already covers you. Asking for consent you didn't need, and then losing most of the list when people ignore the email, is an expensive mistake to make for no reason.

**The fines will get the headlines.** Up to €20 million or 4% of global turnover is a scary number. For most retailers I think the more realistic cost is the work: auditing data, updating privacy notices, handling subject access requests within a month, and changing how forms and tracking work. That needs starting now rather than in May.

**The ePrivacy Regulation will lag behind.** It was meant to arrive alongside GDPR and cover cookies and electronic marketing. It's clearly not going to be ready in time, which leaves a fair bit of uncertainty about cookies and tracking for a while yet.

## The browsers are making their own rules

Separately from the regulators, the browsers are changing what tracking can do.

Apple shipped Intelligent Tracking Prevention in Safari 11 in September. It limits how third-party cookies can be used to follow people across sites, and given how much of mobile traffic comes from iPhones, that's going to affect retargeting and attribution in ways most marketers haven't noticed yet. I expect to see conversion data from Safari users getting quietly worse through 2018, and a lot of confusion about why.

Google has said Chrome will start filtering ads on sites that don't meet the Better Ads Standards early next year. It's aimed at the worst formats: pop-ups, autoplay video with sound, full-screen takeovers. For most advertisers it shouldn't change much directly, but it's the same direction as the interstitial penalty at the start of this year.

## Search

The mobile-first index has been in testing for a year now, and Google says it will roll out gradually. I expect 2018 to be the year it properly lands. If your mobile site has less content, fewer internal links or worse structured data than your desktop one, that gap is going to matter. Responsive sites should be mostly fine; separate m-dot sites are where I'd be checking.

Voice search will keep getting talked about. With the Echo and Google Home both available in the UK now, more people are asking questions out loud. I'm still not convinced it changes much for retail in 2018, beyond making featured snippets more valuable, but I'd rather be watching it than not.

## Data, again

Most of this comes back to knowing what data you collect and why. Marketers have been able to add tags and collect email addresses without much thought for years, and both the regulators and the browsers are now changing that. I don't mind, but the next six months are going to be busy.
