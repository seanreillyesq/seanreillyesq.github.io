Five months on from the start of lockdown, I've been thinking about which brands came out of the spring looking better, and which came out looking worse. Not in sales terms, which nobody outside a company can judge yet, but in how people talk about them.

From what I've seen, people remember what companies did far more than what they said. I can't recall a single one of the "we're all in this together" emails I received.

## The ones that did something

Some of the clearest examples came from the luxury sector, which surprised a few people. In mid-March LVMH turned its perfume and cosmetics production lines over to making hand sanitiser for French hospitals. Burberry retooled its trench coat factory in Yorkshire to make non-surgical gowns and masks for the NHS. Barbour did something similar at its factory in South Shields. Plenty of smaller makers, tailors, leather workshops and textile mills did versions of the same, usually without a press release.

What these had in common was that they used what the company was actually good at. A fashion brand making gowns is believable because it has the machinists and the cutting tables, and it costs the business something. A brand changing its logo or posting a message of support costs nothing.

## The ones that didn't

Everyone can name a few examples from the other side. Sports Direct argued in the first days of lockdown that it should stay open as an essential retailer, and backed down within a day after a public backlash. Several big companies were criticised for furloughing staff while still planning dividends, and some reversed course quickly. Others tried to keep trading in ways their own staff weren't comfortable with, and found that out on social media.

What struck me most was how fast the judgement happened. Within a day or two, a decision made in a boardroom was being argued about on Twitter, and in most of the cases I followed the first version of the story stuck.

## Why luxury brands should care more than most

A luxury or heritage brand sells more than the product. It sells a sense of who made it, how, and what the company stands for. That's what justifies the price. It also makes those brands more exposed when their behaviour doesn't match the story.

If a brand talks about craftsmanship and the people in its workshops, customers will notice how those people were treated when things got difficult. If it talks about community and tradition, they'll notice whether it showed up for its community when it could. None of that needs grand gestures. For most small brands the honest list was fairly short: keep people safe, pay suppliers on time where possible, treat staff decently, and be straight with customers about delays.

## What this means for marketing

I'm wary of the conclusion some people are drawing, which is that every brand now needs a purpose campaign. I don't think that follows at all. Customers can spot a brand that has bolted a cause on to its marketing, and the spring has made them sharper at it.

What I'd take from it is more modest:

- **Do the thing before you talk about it.** The brands that came out well mostly did something first and talked about it later, if at all.
- **Use what you're actually good at.** A shirtmaker with spare capacity can make scrubs; posting about frontline heroes adds very little.
- **Assume everything internal will become public.** How staff are treated is now part of the brand, whether you like it or not.
- **Tell customers the truth about service.** Plenty of brands this spring promised delivery times they couldn't meet. The ones that were honest about delays seem to have been forgiven much more easily.

There's probably a second test coming. Nobody knows what the autumn will bring, but if there's another wave, customers will remember how brands behaved in the first one. I'd like the brands I work with to have thought about it before then.
