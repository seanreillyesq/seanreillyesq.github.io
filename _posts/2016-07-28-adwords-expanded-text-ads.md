Expanded text ads went live for all AdWords advertisers this week. Google announced them at its Performance Summit in May, and after a couple of months of beta they're now the format everyone can use. The old standard text ads will stop being creatable in the autumn; Google's date at the moment is 26 October, after which you'll only be able to edit or pause the old ones, not make new ones.

That gives us three months to rewrite every text ad in every account. Not a glamorous job, and the most hands-on change to search ads I can remember.

## What's changed

The quick version:

- **Two headlines of up to 30 characters each**, shown together, instead of one 25-character headline.
- **One description of up to 80 characters**, replacing the two 35-character lines.
- **The display URL now takes its domain from your final URL**, with two optional "path" fields of up to 15 characters each that you can use to show something like /rolex/submariner.
- **Built for mobile first.** The format is designed around how ads look on a phone, and there's no longer a separate mobile-preferred ad needed in most cases.

Put simply, you get close to double the headline space. Google says that in early testing <a href="https://doubleclick-advertisers.googleblog.com/2016/05/three-new-ways-for-brands-to-succeed.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">some advertisers saw click-through rates up to 20% higher</a> than with the old ads, which I'd treat as a best case, but even a fraction of that is worth having.

It also makes sense alongside the change from February, when Google took ads off the right-hand side of desktop results and moved to up to four ads at the top. Fewer slots, at the top of the page, with more room in each one. The competition for those positions was already fiercer this year, and the ads that win them will take up a lot more of the screen.

## Why copy and paste is a mistake

The tempting approach is to take the old headline, add a second headline that's basically the old description line one, merge the rest into the new description, and call it done. Plenty of accounts will do exactly that, and to be fair it's better than nothing.

The trouble is that the old ads were written around the old constraints. Every word was squeezed into 25 and 35 characters, and the result reads like a telegram. Stitching those fragments together in the new format doesn't fix that.

What I'd rather do is treat it as a rewrite:

1. **Headline one does the matching.** It should reflect the search as closely as possible, because that's still what makes someone feel they've found the right thing.
2. **Headline two does the persuading.** The reason to click you rather than the ad above or below. For us that's things like warranty, authentication and the range of stock, the stuff that matters to someone spending real money.
3. **Write the description as a sentence.** Eighty characters is enough for a proper one.
4. **Use the paths.** A display URL that shows the brand and model reassures someone they'll land on the right page, not the homepage.

## Run them side by side first

Don't switch off the old ads the day you launch the new ones. Run both in the same ad groups for a few weeks, let the data come in, and pause the standard ads once you can see how the new ones compare. Some of your old ads will have years of performance history behind them, and a brand-new ad, however well written, is starting from zero.

Prioritise the campaigns that spend the most. In most accounts a small number of ad groups carry most of the budget, so rewrite those properly first and work down. The long tail of small ad groups can get a more templated treatment if time runs short.

## The bit I'm unsure about

The thing I can't tell yet is how this interacts with ad extensions. Sitelinks, callouts and the rest were partly a way around the tiny old format, and with bigger ads at the top of the page I wonder how often all of them will still show. I'll keep using them, but I'd like to see a few weeks of impression data before deciding how much effort to put into maintaining them.

It's a big job across a large account, and we're starting this week with the highest-spending brand and model campaigns.
