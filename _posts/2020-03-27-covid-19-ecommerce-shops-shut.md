On Monday evening the Prime Minister told everyone to stay at home, and every non-essential shop in the country closed. For brands with physical stores, the website is now the only shop open. I'm writing this at the end of the first week, mostly to get my own thinking straight, and with the caveat that everything I say could look different in a fortnight.

## Can you fulfil safely?

Before anyone talks about campaigns, there's a more basic question: can you actually fulfil orders safely? Warehouse and workshop teams can't work from home, and social distancing in a packing room or a small workshop isn't trivial. Next has paused its online operation entirely to look at exactly this, which tells you how seriously the big retailers are taking it.

If you can't fulfil safely, the honest thing is to say so on the site and stop taking orders, or take them with a clear note about when they'll ship. Taking money for orders you can't send will lose you customers you'll want back later.

## Then the carriers

Assuming you can pack, the next weak point is delivery. Royal Mail and the couriers are running, but everyone expects delays, and international is more patchy, with flights cancelled and some countries restricting what comes in. Delivery promises on product pages and in checkout need checking now. "Next working day" text that was true a fortnight ago probably isn't today.

I'd also look at the order confirmation and dispatch emails, which most of us haven't read since we set them up.

## What to say to customers

My inbox this week is a parade of "a message from our CEO" emails, most of them from companies I barely remember buying from. I don't think anyone needs another one. If you email your list, have something useful to say: whether you're open, how delivery is working, what you're doing for staff. Then stop.

For a luxury brand in particular, I think tone matters more than usual. People are frightened, many are about to lose income, and a hard-sell promotion lands badly. At the same time, plenty of customers are at home, still employed, and will want to buy things. Staying quietly open seems reasonable to me; shouting about it doesn't.

## The paid media question

The instinct in a crisis is to pause all spend. I understand it, and for some businesses it's right, particularly if fulfilment is in doubt. But it's worth thinking about rather than doing by reflex. Ad auctions are getting cheaper as travel, events and hospitality advertisers vanish overnight. If you can fulfil, and your customers are still buying, this may be a period where the same budget goes further.

I'd want to see a couple of weeks of data before deciding either way. Demand for some categories is clearly collapsing, and for others, anything you'd use at home, it seems to be growing. Formal shirts for the office are probably in the first group for a while.

## The people side

The government announced the Coronavirus Job Retention Scheme last Friday: furlough, with the state covering 80% of wages up to £2,500 a month for staff who aren't working. The detail is still being worked out. For small brands with shops that can't open, it's going to be the difference between keeping teams and losing them, and it'll shape who's around to run ecommerce for the next few months. I suspect a lot of small ecommerce teams are about to find out who else in the business can learn to process returns.

## What I'm watching

For now I'm trying to keep a simple daily view:

- orders and revenue against the same days last year, not last week, because last week is already meaningless;
- traffic by channel, to see whether demand is moving rather than disappearing;
- customer service contacts and what they're about;
- stock of the lines people are actually buying.

I'll write more once there's enough data to say something useful, probably in a few weeks.
