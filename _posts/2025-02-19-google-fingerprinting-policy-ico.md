Since Sunday, 16 February, advertisers using Google's ad products are no longer prohibited from using device fingerprinting. Google announced the change in December and it has now taken effect.

If you haven't followed this: fingerprinting is the practice of identifying a device from a combination of signals it gives off. Screen size, fonts, browser version, time zone, IP address, graphics card quirks. None of them identifies you alone. Combined, they often do. And unlike a cookie, the user can't see it, can't clear it and mostly can't block it.

Google itself used to say this was wrong. In 2019 it described fingerprinting as something that "subverts user choice". So the reversal is quite a turn.

## The ICO was not impressed

The UK regulator responded in December with a blog post calling the change <a href="https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2024/12/our-response-to-google-s-policy-change-on-fingerprinting/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">irresponsible</a>. The line I'd underline for anyone in marketing is that businesses "do not have free rein to use fingerprinting as they please". It's still covered by PECR and UK GDPR. You still need consent. You still need to be transparent about it. And because the user can't easily see or switch it off, the ICO's view is that meeting those requirements is a high bar.

So the position in the UK is fairly simple. Google's policy no longer stops you. The law still does, or at least makes it very hard to do properly.

## Why Google did it

Google's own reasoning points at connected TVs, consoles and other surfaces where cookies don't really exist, and at privacy-enhancing technologies that supposedly let you match audiences without exposing individuals. There's something to that. Measuring ads on a smart TV is genuinely awkward.

I'd also read it alongside last July's decision to keep third-party cookies in Chrome. The industry has spent five years being told a cookieless future was coming, built for it, and then watched it get postponed indefinitely. Loosening the fingerprinting rule gives ad tech another identifier to work with. Whether that's good for anyone apart from ad tech is another question.

## Why it matters more than it sounds

The thing that makes fingerprinting different from cookies is the loss of the off switch. A user who clears cookies, uses a private window or rejects a banner has made a choice and, broadly, the cookie world respects it. A fingerprint survives all of that. Advertisers like it for that reason, and regulators dislike it for the same one. If your brand's tracking only works because the customer can't stop it, that's a difficult thing to explain if it ever ends up in the press.

## What I'd do if I were advertising in the UK

- **Ask your vendors.** If you work with DSPs, measurement partners or retargeting platforms, ask them in writing whether they use fingerprinting or probabilistic device matching, and on what legal basis. Some will be cagey. Write that down too.
- **Check your consent setup covers it.** Most cookie banners talk about cookies. If something on your site is fingerprinting, your banner probably doesn't describe it, which means you don't have valid consent for it.
- **Don't rush to adopt it.** There's no competitive prize for being first to use a technique the regulator has publicly warned about. Your brand carries the reputational risk, not the vendor.
- **Keep investing in first-party data.** Logged-in customers, email, a reason for people to tell you who they are. It's slower and less exciting, but you control it.

What I don't know is how hard the ICO will actually push. It has said it will engage with Google and look at compliance, and it's published draft guidance on storage and access technologies that covers fingerprinting explicitly. Enforcement in this area has historically been slow. But I wouldn't want to rely on the regulator being slow.

For most UK advertisers the practical answer is to change nothing on your own site, and to ask some pointed questions of the platforms you pay.
