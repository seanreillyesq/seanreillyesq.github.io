Two bits of news this month have pushed first-party data back to the top of my list. On Tuesday Google dropped FLoC and announced the Topics API as its replacement idea for interest-based advertising in Chrome. And on the 13th, noyb published a decision from the Austrian data protection authority finding that a website using Google Analytics had breached GDPR, because the data could end up in the US.

Neither changes anything on my sites tomorrow morning. Chrome still accepts third-party cookies, and Google's current timeline has them phased out from late 2023. GA is still running. But the direction is about as clear as it ever gets in this industry, and the people who will cope best are the ones who start collecting their own data properly while the old way still works alongside it.

## What I mean by first-party data

The phrase gets stretched to mean almost anything, so here is the plain version. It's information your customers give you directly, on your own properties, with their knowledge:

- Purchase history and what they browsed before buying
- Email sign-ups and the preferences they set
- Account data, sizes, saved addresses, wishlists
- Answers to anything you ask them, from a post-purchase survey to "how did you hear about us?"

None of it relies on a cookie set by somebody else's domain, which is the appeal.

## Why ecommerce should care more than most

For an online shop the obvious hit from losing third-party cookies is retargeting and prospecting audiences. Anyone who lived through iOS 14.5 last year has had a preview. Once Apple let people opt out of tracking, Facebook's reported conversions dropped, the lookalike audiences got woollier, and a lot of us discovered how much of our "performance" depended on data we never owned.

Apple's Mail Privacy Protection in iOS 15 did something similar to email. Open rates are now inflated for anyone reading in Apple Mail, so the metric half the industry used to judge subject lines has stopped telling the truth, at least for that part of your list.

So the cookie change arrives on top of two other knocks in under a year, and all of them hurt most where a business relied on an ad platform to understand its own customers.

## Where I'd start

I'd resist the urge to buy a customer data platform first. CDPs have their place, but I've seen plenty of teams spend a year integrating one and end up with a very expensive way of storing the same thin data they had before. I'd sort out what goes into it before worrying about where it's stored.

The order I'd work in:

1. **Get the sign-up right.** Give people a real reason to hand over an email address, and ask for one or two preferences while you have their attention. Not twelve.
2. **Fix the account experience.** If creating an account is a chore, people check out as guests and you learn nothing about them next time.
3. **Ask the question analytics can't answer.** A single "how did you hear about us?" field at checkout tells you things no attribution model will. The answers are messy, and I'd still rather have them.
4. **Tidy consent.** Make sure what people agreed to is recorded properly and that you can prove it. The Austrian ruling is a reminder that regulators are reading this stuff now.
5. **Then connect it.** Once there's decent data flowing in, look at how your email platform, ecommerce platform and analytics talk to each other.

## The honest downside

First-party data is slow. You are building something that pays back over years, and in the meantime it looks like admin. It also only covers people who already know you, which is why it complements prospecting rather than replacing it. Nobody I know has worked out how to find genuinely new customers at scale once the third-party signals thin out, and I'm wary of anyone who claims they have.

I'll keep an eye on how Topics develops and whether other regulators follow Austria's lead. For now I'm treating late 2023 as a hard deadline, even though I half expect Google to move it again.
