Nobody in luxury ecommerce needs convincing that photography matters. I made the case last year in a post about [selling craftsmanship online](/selling-craftsmanship-online-20180918/), and I haven't met anyone who disagrees. Where the disagreements start is in the practical detail: who takes the pictures, how many, how they stay consistent, and how you stop a gallery of beautiful images turning your product pages into treacle on a phone.

These are the questions I've spent more time on than I expected, so here are some notes.

## Keeping it consistent

A single stunning photograph is easy. A catalogue of several hundred products shot over two years by different people in different light, all looking like they belong together, is much harder, and I think it does more for how expensive a site looks.

The thing that helps most is a written shot list and style guide, boring as that sounds. Same background, same angles in the same order, same crop, same distance from the camera for the same type of product. If shirts are always shown front, back, collar detail, cuff detail and on a model, a customer learns where to look. If every product page has a different set, they have to work it out each time.

## Getting the colour right

Most "not as pictured" returns in clothing and leather come down to colour. Navy that photographs as black, a tan bag that looks orange on one screen and brown on another. You can't control the customer's screen, but you can control everything up to that point:

- Shoot with a colour checker card in the frame for every new setup.
- Calibrate the monitor the editing is done on.
- Compare the edited image against the physical product, side by side, in daylight, before it goes live.

That last step takes ten minutes per product and gets skipped constantly. I'd make it someone's job.

## In-house or studio

Sending everything to an external studio produces lovely, consistent results, and it's the right choice for a seasonal launch. The trouble is the turnaround. When a new cloth arrives on a Tuesday and the shop could sell it on Wednesday, waiting two weeks for studio images means two weeks of not selling it online.

The approach I like is a mix: studio for hero and campaign imagery, and a small, well set-up in-house kit for everyday product shots, following the same style guide. A decent camera, a couple of continuous lights, a roll of background paper and a table don't cost a great deal, and the gain in speed pays for them quickly. The style guide is what stops the in-house shots looking like the poor relation.

## Weight and speed

This is the bit that gets forgotten. High-resolution images are what make zoom work, and zoom is essential when the selling point is stitching or grain. But serving a 3MB image to a phone on a train is a good way to lose the customer before they see any of it.

The answer is to serve the right size to the right device, which most modern platforms and image CDNs will do if you let them. Upload the large master, let the platform produce the smaller versions, lazy-load the images below the fold, and only fetch the zoom-resolution version when someone actually zooms. I wrote about [Google's Speed Update](/google-speed-update-ecommerce-20180717/) last summer; the ranking effect is small, but the conversion effect of slow product pages isn't.

## Images are found too

People search Google Images for products more than many brands realise, especially for things that are bought on looks. Google's redesign of image search last autumn put more of the surrounding page context next to each image, which makes the page itself matter more. A few habits help:

- Descriptive file names rather than IMG_4471.jpg.
- Alt text that describes the product plainly, the way you'd describe it to someone over the phone.
- Images placed near the text that describes them, on a page with a sensible title.

Mostly housekeeping, but it means people can actually find the photography you've paid for.

## Is it worth the money?

Good photography is one of the bigger recurring costs in a small luxury ecommerce operation, and it's a fair question to ask. I find it hard to put a number on it, because there's rarely a clean test. But I'd point to two things that are measurable: returns attributed to colour or appearance, and the conversion rate of products with a full gallery against those with one or two images. If those numbers don't move when the photography improves, then something else is wrong.
