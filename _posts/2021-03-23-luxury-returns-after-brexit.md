Returns are the part of ecommerce nobody puts in the pitch deck. Everyone talks about acquisition and conversion, and then a parcel comes back three weeks later and quietly undoes the margin on the sale. In luxury the stakes on each parcel are higher, and since 1 January the EU side of it has become genuinely messy.

## Why high-value returns are different

On a £20 t-shirt a return is an irritation. On a handmade bag or a bespoke-adjacent shirt it's a different problem, for a few reasons.

- **Condition matters more.** A returned item has to be inspected properly before it can go back on sale. Leather marks, creases, a faint smell of perfume. Someone who knows the product has to look at it, which takes time and skill.
- **Some items can't really be resold as new.** Personalised and made-to-order pieces are the obvious case, and they need clear, honest terms up front. The Consumer Contracts Regulations already allow exemptions for goods made to the customer's specification, but the customer needs to understand that before they pay, not after.
- **Wear-and-return exists.** Every retailer in fashion knows this. It's a small minority, but on a high-value item one bad return can wipe out the profit on several good sales.
- **Customers still expect free, easy returns.** Pandemic shopping has hardened that expectation, because returning by post has been the only way.

So the policy ends up a balancing act. Too strict and you lose the sale, because a nervous customer buying a few hundred pounds of something they can't touch wants to know they can send it back. Too generous and a handful of people treat it as a free rental service.

## What Brexit changed

Since the transition period ended, goods going from the UK to EU customers are imports. That brings import VAT and, depending on rules of origin, customs duty. If the retailer doesn't handle that up front, the customer gets a bill from the courier on the doorstep before they're handed the parcel. Plenty of people have refused delivery rather than pay it, and some UK retailers paused EU shipping entirely in January while they worked out what to do.

Returns make it worse. When an EU customer sends something back, it's now an export from their side and an import on ours. Getting relief on duty and VAT for returned goods needs the right paperwork, and the couriers I've seen aren't consistent about it yet. The customer who paid import charges also wants those back, and recovering them isn't always straightforward.

There's more change coming. The EU's new VAT rules for ecommerce start on 1 July, including the Import One-Stop Shop for consignments up to €150. Most luxury orders will sit above that threshold, so it won't help much in this corner of the market.

## How I'm thinking about it

None of this is solved, and I'd be wary of anyone who says otherwise this early. But this is where I've got to.

1. **Decide who pays duties, and say so on the product page.** Delivered duty paid, where the price the EU customer sees includes everything, is the better experience. It costs more to set up and you carry the risk. Delivered duty unpaid is cheaper for you and worse for them. Either way, hiding it until the doorstep is the worst option.
2. **Write the returns policy for the customer who's nervous, not the one who's abusing it.** Clear windows, clear condition requirements, clear exceptions for made-to-order. Deal with the abuse case through inspection and, if necessary, by declining future orders, rather than making everyone pay for it.
3. **Make returns data visible.** Return rates by product, by size, by country. A product with a high return rate is usually telling you something about its description, photography or sizing guidance, and that's fixable.
4. **Look at exchange-first flows.** Offering an exchange or store credit before a refund keeps the revenue more often than you'd think, especially for sizing issues.
5. **Talk to the courier now.** Find out exactly what they need for a returned-goods declaration and put it in the customer's returns pack.

Cross-border luxury ecommerce got harder on 1 January and returns are where I'm feeling it most. If it comes to it, I'd accept a smaller EU business over one that keeps generating angry emails about customs bills.
