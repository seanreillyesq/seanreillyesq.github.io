Two changes from Google in one week, and one of them came with something new: advance notice.

On Sunday 2 June, Google's Search Liaison account said a broad core update would start rolling out the next day. As far as I can remember, that's the first time Google has told anyone before a core update rather than confirming it afterwards. Then on 6 June it announced a separate change aimed at showing a greater diversity of sites in the results.

They're different things, and they're getting mixed together in a lot of the commentary, so I'll take them in turn.

## The June 2019 Core Update

On the core update itself there's not a lot new to say. Same name format as [the March update](/march-2019-core-update-20190320/), same guidance: no specific fix, focus on content quality, read the rater guidelines.

The early movement looks similar to previous rounds, with a lot of activity among health, finance and news sites. A large UK news publisher has been unusually open about losing a big share of its search traffic, which has got plenty of attention because publishers rarely talk about these things in public. As ever, a few days in is too early for confident conclusions.

The interesting part is the pre-announcement. I read it as Google trying to stop the guessing game. When an update arrives unannounced, the first few days are filled with speculation about what's happening and whether it's a penalty. Saying "this is a core update, and it starts tomorrow" takes some of the heat out of that. It also makes it much easier to answer the question every in-house SEO gets asked: was it us, or was it Google? If the drop starts on the 3rd and you made no changes on the 3rd, you have a fairly clear answer.

The quality rater guidelines were also updated in May, with more detail on E-A-T and on the reputation of content creators. Google always says the guidelines don't directly feed the algorithm, but they tell you what Google is trying to reward, and that's worth reading.

## The site diversity change

This one is more concrete. Google says that for most searches it will now generally show no more than two results from the same site in the top results, unless it thinks more are particularly relevant. It also said subdomains will generally be treated as part of the main domain for this purpose.

Anyone who has searched for a product and found five results from the same retailer filling the first page will see why. For users, it should mean more variety. For site owners, the effect depends on where you sit:

- **Large sites that held several positions for one query** may lose some of them. In practice, the second and third result from the same site were often getting little traffic anyway, so the loss may be smaller than it looks in a rank tracker.
- **Smaller sites** might pick up the freed positions, though Google has been clear that this isn't a guarantee.
- **Subdomain tactics**, putting content on a subdomain to get a second listing, are less likely to work. They were a fragile idea already.

Early reports suggest the effect is modest so far. I've seen plenty of searches that still show three results from one domain, which fits with the "generally" in Google's wording.

## For smaller ecommerce sites

For a small luxury brand, neither change calls for much action. If anything the diversity change helps slightly; specialist sites competing against large multi-brand retailers might find the big players occupying a bit less of the first page.

The more useful takeaway is the pre-announcement. If Google keeps doing it, I'd add Search Liaison to whatever you use to keep an eye on things, and note update dates alongside your own release log. A known start date makes it much quicker to rule Google in or out when traffic drops.
