<p class="lead">Ten years ago I decided I would walk the whole North Downs Way, a section at a time, whenever life let me. Life, it turned out, had plans of its own.</p>

Between then and now there was a house, a couple of jobs, two children growing up, and a pandemic. Ten years back a few of us had walked the Canterbury sections - [east to Dover](/north-downs-way-canterbury-to-dover-20110918/) one Sunday, [round to Wye and back](/north-downs-way-canterbury-wye-loop-20111016/) another - and then the plan quietly stalled. The trail sat there the whole time, patient, getting no shorter. So this morning I picked it back up where there was an easy gap: Wye to Folkestone, the other stretch that carries the path down to the sea.

There was a proper reason for going, beyond guilt. I've run this sort of distance before, grinding out the miles years ago training for the London Marathon. But I had never walked as far on my own, and lately I've been dreaming about bigger things: proper multi-day routes, a tent or a bunkhouse, days on end in the hills by myself. Before any of that, it seemed sensible to find out whether I could actually enjoy a long day on my own two feet with only my own company. Wye to Folkestone was the test.

The forecast looked kind, so I drove over to Wye first thing, left the car, and talked Nicole into rescuing me from the far end later on.

The walk starts by going straight up. From Wye you climb hard onto the escarpment, past the Wye Crown cut into the hillside and along the lip of the Devil's Kneading Trough, a great green scoop out of the downs that is far more dramatic than anything in Kent has much right to be. After that it settles into the thing the North Downs Way does best: mile after mile of high, open ground with half the county laid out on one side.

Somewhere along the top I passed a waymark I remembered from the walk to Dover: the little blue E2 badge. The path I was on runs, in theory, all the way to the Mediterranean; it even shares a stretch with the Tour du Mont Blanc, which I walked years ago. I was only going as far as Folkestone. 

The earlier sections I'd walked were part of the Canterbury Loop, the trail's longer arm that threads together ancient roads: the Pilgrims' Way, the Via Francigena. This stretch is different. Wye to Folkestone is on the Mainline Route, the direct line down to the coast, and rather than following one old road it was stitched together from many when the North Downs Way opened as a National Trail in 1978. 

From Tolsford Hill onward the path shares its line with the Saxon Shore Way, which traces the Kent coast as it stood in Roman times: keeping to the old mainland and steering well wide of the Isle of Thanet and Romney Marsh, both of which have joined the coast in the centuries since, as the sea silted up behind them. 

The two paths meet under the great concrete BT tower on Tolsford Hill, two hundred and twenty feet of it on the highest ground for miles. By the time I passed it I felt good. Really good, in fact: strong and unhurried, eating up the miles, quietly pleased with myself and this whole solo-walking business.

That lasted until a man went past me as though I were a lamp post. A cheerful Nepalese chap, he chatted for a moment and mentioned, in passing, that he was sixty, just retired, and - I could have guessed - a former Gurkha. Then he was gone up the hill, and I was left reconsidering my earlier smugness. There is nothing like being soundly out-walked by someone nearly twenty years your senior to put a day back in proportion.

The sky stayed grey the whole way, that flat Kentish overcast, but it never rained and I didn't much care. After a year and a bit of lockdowns and the walls of the house, being out on real hills with real distance in front of me was the entire point. Grey was fine. Grey was wonderful.

The last few miles tip down off the downs towards the coast, and Folkestone gathers itself below you: the town, the harbour, the sea going flat and pale to the horizon. I stayed high above the Channel Tunnel and on to Caesar's Camp, a great tangle of banks and ditches that, despite the Roman name, is really a Norman ringwork castle - the most complete one in the south-east. From there the whole coast opens up below you. Then I dropped down through the park to Nicole and the car, and the very welcome prospect of not having to walk back.

Eighteen miles, the furthest I've ever walked in one go, and no pub stops to break it up. The Merrells from the early walks are long gone; these are the Moab 2s, and they did the job without a word of complaint. The legs are tired in the good way.

So I have my answer. I can do a long day on my own, and I want more of them - somewhere bigger and wilder, several strung together. The Gurkha can come too, as long as he promises to stay behind me.
