<p class="lead">Ten years ago I decided I would walk the whole North Downs Way, a section at a time, whenever life let me. Life, it turned out, had plans of its own.</p>

Between then and now there was a house, a couple of jobs, three children growing up, and a pandemic. Ten years back a few of us had walked the Canterbury sections - [east to Dover](/north-downs-way-canterbury-to-dover-20110918/) one Sunday, [round to Wye and back](/north-downs-way-canterbury-wye-loop-20111016/) another - and then the plan quietly stalled. The trail sat there the whole time, patient, getting no shorter. So this morning I picked it back up where the gap was: Wye to Folkestone, the stretch that carries the path down to the sea.

There was a proper reason for going, beyond guilt. I've run this sort of distance before, grinding out the miles years ago training for the London Marathon. But I had never walked it, not on my own, and lately I've been dreaming about bigger things: proper multi-day routes, a tent or a bunkhouse, days on end in the hills by myself. Before any of that, it seemed sensible to find out whether I could actually enjoy a long day on my own two feet with only my own company for it. Wye to Folkestone was the test.

The forecast looked kind, so I drove over to Wye first thing, left the car, and talked Nicole into rescuing me from the far end later on.

The walk starts by going straight up. From Wye you climb hard onto the escarpment, past the Wye Crown cut into the hillside and along the lip of the Devil's Kneading Trough, a great green scoop out of the downs that is far more dramatic than anything in Kent has much right to be. After that it settles into the thing the North Downs Way does best: mile after mile of high, open ground with half the county laid out on one side.

Somewhere along the top I passed a waymark I remembered from the walk to Dover: the little blue E2 badge. The path I was on runs, in theory, all the way to the Mediterranean. I was going to Folkestone.

I felt good. Really good, in fact; strong and unhurried, eating up the miles, quietly pleased with myself and this whole solo-walking business.

That lasted until a man went past me as though I were a lamp post. He was cheerful about it, chatted for a moment, and mentioned in passing that he was fifty-five, retired, and had spent his career as a Gurkha. Then he was off up the hill and I was left reconsidering my earlier smugness. There is nothing like being soundly out-walked by a pensioner to put a day back in proportion.

The sky stayed grey the whole way, that flat Kentish overcast, but it never rained and I didn't much care. After a year and a bit of lockdowns and the walls of the house, being out on real hills with real distance in front of me was the entire point. Grey was fine. Grey was wonderful.

The last few miles tip you off the downs towards the coast, and Folkestone gathers itself below you: the town, the harbour, the sea going flat and pale to the horizon. I came down through the Folkestone Downs, past the viewpoint where the whole coast opens up below you, and finished on the Harbour Arm with Nicole and the car, and the very welcome prospect of not having to walk back.

Eighteen miles, the furthest I've ever walked in one go. The Merrells from the early walks are long gone; these are the Moab 2s and they did the job without a word of complaint. The legs are tired in the good way.

So I have my answer. I can do a long day on my own, and I want more of them - somewhere bigger and wilder, several strung together. The Gurkha can come too, as long as he promises to stay behind me.
