Google started rolling out the March 2024 core update a week ago, on 5 March, alongside a spam update and three new spam policies. It's the biggest set of changes to Search in a while, and Google said so itself, which it doesn't usually do. The announcement talked about cutting low-quality, unoriginal content in results by 40%.

A week in, here's what I understand has changed and what I'd look at.

## Helpful content is now part of the core

The helpful content system, which launched in August 2022 as a separate site-wide signal, no longer exists as a separate thing. Google says it has been folded into the core ranking systems, and that there won't be standalone helpful content updates any more.

In practice that means the site-level judgement about whether your content was written for people or for search engines now moves with every core update, rather than in its own occasional bursts. The sites that were hit by the September 2023 helpful content update have been waiting for a chance to recover. This is the chance, though Google has been careful not to promise anything.

Google also said this update is more complex than usual and could take up to a month to finish. Rankings are going to move around for a while. I wouldn't read much into any single week of data until it's done.

## The three new spam policies

These are the more interesting part to me, because they're specific.

**Scaled content abuse.** Producing large amounts of unoriginal content mainly to rank, whatever method you use to make it. The previous policy was framed around automatically generated content. The new wording deliberately covers AI, human writers churning out templated pages, or a mix, so the method doesn't get you off the hook.

**Site reputation abuse.** Third-party pages hosted on a trusted site to borrow its ranking power, with little oversight from the host. The examples are things like payday loan or casino reviews appearing on a news site or a university subdomain. Google is giving two months' notice on this one, with enforcement starting on 5 May, which suggests a fair few well-known publishers have some tidying to do.

**Expired domain abuse.** Buying an expired domain for its old links and reputation, then filling it with low-value content unrelated to what it used to be. Anyone who has looked at a backlink profile in the last ten years will have seen these sites. Plenty of SEOs have been buying expired domains for exactly this, so I expect some squealing.

## The manual actions

The part that has got most attention in the first week isn't the algorithm at all. A wave of manual actions went out in the first few days, and a lot of sites were removed from the index entirely, not just demoted. From the lists being shared on X, many of them look like exactly what you'd expect: sites that went from a few hundred pages to many thousands in 2023, most of it AI-written, monetised with display ads.

That's a reminder that Google does still use people for this, alongside the algorithm.

## What I'd check

If traffic has dropped this week:

1. **Look at Search Console for a manual action first.** It's under Security and Manual Actions. If there's one there, that's your problem, and no amount of waiting fixes it.
2. **Check which pages lost visibility.** A site-wide drop is a different conversation from a handful of templates losing out. If it's your location pages or your auto-generated category combinations, the scaled content policy is the obvious suspect.
3. **Audit anything hosted on your domain that you don't control.** Sponsored sections, partner content, coupon pages run by a third party. If it's there to rank on your reputation, you've got until May.
4. **Wait for the rollout to finish before rewriting everything.** Panic-editing hundreds of pages in the middle of a core update leaves you with no idea which change did what.

If traffic has gone up, don't celebrate too early either. Early movement in a long rollout often reverses.

## My view

I'm broadly in favour. Search quality has visibly slipped over the last eighteen months, and a lot of that slippage has been cheap AI content and parasite pages on big domains. The 40% figure is a claim, and we'll only know whether results feel better once the update settles. But the policies themselves are clearer than anything Google has published on spam in years, and they describe things that were obviously wrong already.

For anyone producing content properly, with real expertise and an actual reason for each page to exist, the advice is the same boring advice. Keep doing that, and check your site doesn't have any old corners that look like the things Google has just described.
