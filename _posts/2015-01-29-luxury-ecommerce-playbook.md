I spend a fair amount of time looking at other people's websites, mostly out of habit, and over the last few months I've been paying attention to luxury retail online. The thing that jumps out is how much of the standard ecommerce toolkit those sites simply don't use. At first I assumed they were behind. The longer I looked, the more I came round to thinking most of them have made a deliberate call, and a sensible one.

## The standard toolkit

If you've worked on a mass-market ecommerce site in the last few years you'll know the moves. Most of them come out of conversion rate testing and most of them do lift the numbers:

- a pop-up offering 10% off your first order in exchange for an email address
- countdown timers on offers ("ends in 03:12:44")
- scarcity flags ("only 2 left in stock", "14 people are looking at this")
- free delivery thresholds nudging the basket up
- a basket abandonment email within the hour, then another, then a discount code

None of this is sinister. On a site selling phone cases or bedding it's just good practice, and I've recommended most of it myself at one time or another.

## Why it misfires at the top end

Run the same list past a luxury purchase and nearly every item backfires.

The discount pop-up is the obvious one. On a £20 order, 10% off is a nice welcome. On a £3,000 handbag it does two things at once: it tells the customer the price is negotiable, and it tells them this is the kind of shop that hands out vouchers. I can't think of a luxury brand that wants either thought in a customer's head.

Urgency is worse, because people don't buy expensive things under pressure. A big purchase is considered, often over weeks, sometimes discussed with a partner, looked at in person, come back to. A ticking clock on the screen reads as a trick to someone in that frame of mind, and the luxury shopper is often a fairly sophisticated one who has seen every trick going.

Scarcity is interesting because luxury genuinely runs on it. But in luxury it tends to show up as waiting lists, limited editions and the occasional raised eyebrow from a sales assistant. A red badge counting down stock levels makes it feel more like a supermarket promotion.

And the email cadence that works for fast fashion can feel like being chased down the street. A customer who browsed an expensive item once does not need three emails by Thursday.

## What luxury sites do instead

The better luxury sites I've been looking at put their effort into a different set of things:

- **Imagery that does the selling.** Big, slow-loading-but-worth-it photography, detail shots, sometimes video. The product page reads more like a magazine page than a listing.
- **Service in the foreground.** Phone numbers you can actually see, appointment booking, a named contact, gift wrapping. The site tells you a human is available.
- **Restraint.** Lots of white space, few calls to action, no flashing anything.
- **Content around the product.** Stories about craft, heritage, how something is made. Slower to produce, harder to measure, and much more in keeping with how these purchases are made.

Some of the biggest houses still won't sell their core products online at all, which tells you how seriously they take the risk of the channel cheapening them.

## The measurement trap

The difficulty for a marketer is that the standard tactics are easy to test and the luxury alternatives aren't. Add a countdown timer, run an A/B test for a fortnight, and you'll probably see a short-term lift. What the test won't show you is what that timer did to how the brand is perceived, or whether the customer who bought this month comes back next year. With high price points and customers who can be worth a lot over several years, I'd argue the second question matters more.

I think this is where a lot of digital people get luxury wrong. We're trained to optimise what we can measure this week, and in luxury the thing that matters most tends to show up a year later, if it shows up in the data at all.

I'm not saying luxury sites shouldn't test. Plenty of them could do with being far more rigorous about checkout friction, page speed and mobile, which is where most of the conversion is genuinely lost. My reservations are about the persuasion tactics layered on top.

## Where I've landed

My rule of thumb is a simple question: would a good sales assistant in the flagship store do this? Would they offer a stranger 10% off before saying hello? Would they hold up a sign saying the offer ends in three hours? If not, it probably doesn't belong on the website either.

It's not a perfect test and I'm sure there are exceptions, but it catches most of the obvious mistakes.
