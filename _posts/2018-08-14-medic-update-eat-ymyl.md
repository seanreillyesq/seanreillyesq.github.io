On 1 August Google confirmed it had released a broad core algorithm update. Within a few days Barry Schwartz at Search Engine Roundtable had nicknamed it the "Medic" update, because so many of the sites reporting big drops were in health, medicine and wellness. Finance, legal and other sites dealing with serious decisions seem to have been hit too, and plenty of sites outside those areas moved as well, in both directions.

Google's response was the one it has been giving to every core update this year. There's nothing specific to fix. Some pages that were previously under-rewarded are now doing better, and if yours dropped it doesn't necessarily mean anything is wrong with it. Danny Sullivan, Google's public search liaison, also pointed people towards the Search Quality Rater Guidelines as a way to understand what Google is aiming for.

He also said there's nothing to fix, which is a hard thing to hear if you've just lost a third of your traffic, but probably true. Either way, most of the SEO conversation since has been about the rater guidelines.

## E-A-T and YMYL

The rater guidelines are the document Google gives to the thousands of human raters who assess the quality of search results. The raters don't directly change rankings; their assessments are used to evaluate whether changes to the algorithm are producing better results. So the guidelines are best read as a description of what Google wants its algorithm to reward.

Two concepts from that document are now everywhere:

- **E-A-T** stands for Expertise, Authoritativeness and Trustworthiness. Raters are asked to judge how much of each a page, its content and its creator have.
- **YMYL** stands for "Your Money or Your Life". These are pages that could affect someone's health, finances, safety or happiness. Raters are told to hold them to a much higher standard.

Neither term is new. Both have been in the guidelines for years. What changed is that the guidelines were updated in late July, with more emphasis on the reputation of the people who actually create the content, and then a few days later a core update landed that seemed to hit exactly the kind of sites the guidelines are strictest about. People joined the dots.

The July revision is worth reading if you have an afternoon. Raters are now asked to look at the reputation of whoever created the main content, not only the website it sits on. There's also more on beneficial purpose: pages created only to make money, with no real attempt to help the person reading, should get the lowest rating. None of that is a ranking factor you can measure. It does describe a lot of the sites that seem to have dropped.

## What I'd take from it

I'm wary of anyone who claims to know precisely what changed. Google uses hundreds of signals and the guidelines are a description of intent, not a list of ranking factors. You can't add "E-A-T" to a page like you add a title tag. But the direction is clear enough to act on.

**Make it obvious who is behind the content.** Author names, credentials where they matter, a proper About page, contact details that work. A health article written anonymously by "Admin" is going to struggle to convince anyone, human or machine.

**Look at your reputation off-site.** The guidelines tell raters to search for reviews and independent information about a business or author. Whatever turns up when someone researches you is part of the picture.

**Be honest about what your site is for.** A lot of the losers seem to be sites mixing commercial product pages with health claims that wouldn't survive scrutiny. If your content exists mainly to sell something, the advice in it needs to be defensible.

**Fix the basics of trust.** Clear returns policies, secure checkout, real customer service details, no surprises. For a shop these are YMYL pages too, because people are handing over money and card details.

## For ecommerce

The ecommerce angle gets less attention than the health one, but the rater guidelines are quite explicit that shopping pages fall under YMYL.

In practice I don't think most well-run online shops have much to fear from this. The sites that look most exposed are ones that have been publishing thin content at scale without any real authority behind it. If your product pages are written by people who know the products, your customer service is reachable, and your reputation holds up when someone searches for it, you're broadly on the right side of where Google is heading.

If you did drop, I'd resist the urge to rewrite everything this week. Read the guidelines, look honestly at your site against them, and look at who is now ranking above you and why. Core updates seem to be coming every few months now, so if the underlying work is right there should be a chance to recover at the next one.
