After a few years in-house, where every decision was about one site, one audience and one set of competitors, agency work means switching sector several times a day. Morning on one industry, afternoon on something completely different. It's been a useful jolt. It makes you work out which bits of SEO are actually universal and which ones you'd only assumed were, because they worked on the one site you knew.

This is my rough list so far.

## What travels well

**Technical foundations.** Crawlability, indexation, sensible URL structures, canonical tags that point where you think they point, a sitemap that reflects reality. None of this cares what you sell. A faceted navigation generating forty thousand near-duplicate URLs is the same problem whether the facets are shoe sizes or holiday durations. Moving between sectors actually helps here, you start recognising the same failures within minutes.

**Titles and descriptions.** Every site has too many of them and most are written in a hurry. The rules for writing a good one barely change between industries, and the most common mistake doesn't either - writing to a character count instead of checking what actually shows. That's the reason I built the [SERP preview tool](/serp-preview-launch-20180213/) earlier this month.

**The basics of matching intent.** Someone searching "how to" wants an explanation; someone searching a product name plus "price" wants to buy or compare. That logic holds everywhere.

## What doesn't

**What counts as a good page.** This is the one that catches people out. In some sectors the best result is a short page with a phone number and opening hours. In others Google is clearly rewarding long, detailed guides, and a thin page won't get a look in however well it's optimised. You can't carry a content template from one sector to another and expect it to work. You have to look at what's actually ranking for the queries that matter and work backwards.

**How competitive "competitive" is.** A keyword that would be a quick win in one industry is a two-year project in another, where every result on page one belongs to a brand with a decade of links behind it. Setting expectations properly depends entirely on the sector, and generic benchmarks are close to useless here.

**Links.** The tactics that earn links in one industry can look odd or even spammy in another. A data-led piece that journalists pick up works in some sectors; in others the realistic sources of links are trade associations, suppliers and local press.

**Who the reader is.** B2B buyers research in committees, over months. Someone buying on impulse decides in minutes. A keyword spreadsheet tells you very little about either.

## The client education bit

The harder part of multi-sector work is often explaining all of this. A client in one industry will have read an article about what worked for a completely different kind of business, and want to know why we aren't doing the same. Sometimes they're right and it would transfer. Often it wouldn't, and the honest answer is "that worked for them because of how their market behaves, and yours behaves differently".

What I've found helps is showing the search results themselves. Pulling up the actual page one for their main terms and asking "what do these pages have in common?" does more than any amount of explanation from me. They vary between sectors far more than most clients expect.

## Where I've landed

Treat the technical work as a checklist you can reuse anywhere. Treat content and links as something you research fresh for each sector, every time, even when it feels like you've done it before. When you're busy the temptation is to reuse the last plan that worked, and I'm trying not to.
