On Monday a US federal judge, Amit Mehta, ruled that Google is a monopolist in general search and in search text advertising, and that it broke the law to keep that position. The line from the opinion that has been quoted everywhere is short: "Google is a monopolist, and it has acted as one to maintain its monopoly."

It's the biggest antitrust ruling against a tech company in over twenty years, and the reaction has ranged from "Google is finished" to "nothing will change". Both seem unlikely to me. The interesting part for anyone who spends money on search is what the court actually found, because it says a lot about the market we've been buying in.

## What the court found

The case centred on default search agreements. Google pays Apple, Samsung, Mozilla, mobile networks and others to be the default search engine on their browsers and devices. The ruling puts the total at more than $26 billion in 2021 alone, with the largest share going to Apple for Safari.

The judge's view was that those deals shut rivals out. Defaults matter enormously because most people never change them, and if every major access point is already locked up, a competitor can't get the scale it needs to build a better product. Google's argument was that people choose it because it's the best search engine. The court accepted that Google is a good product, but found the deals unlawful anyway.

The part I'd pay most attention to as an advertiser is the finding on text ads. The opinion found that Google has been able to raise the prices of search text ads without losing business, because advertisers had nowhere comparable to go. Anyone who has watched their cost per click creep up year after year, while being told it was the auction, will read that paragraph with interest.

## What happens next

Nothing changes this month. The next stage is remedies, where the court decides what Google has to do, and that looks likely to run well into next year. Google has already said it will appeal, and appeals in cases like this take a long time. Realistically we're talking about years before anything material reaches the search results page or the Google Ads interface.

The remedies on the table are anyone's guess at this point. Ending or restricting exclusive default payments is the obvious one. Choice screens, like the one Android users in Europe already see, are another. Some commentators are talking about structural break-ups. I wouldn't bet on any of them yet.

There's a second case to watch too. The US Department of Justice's case against Google's advertising technology business goes to trial in Virginia next month, and that one is closer to the plumbing of display advertising.

## What could change for marketers

If default deals end or get weakened, a few things follow.

- **Bing and others could gain share.** If Safari's default changes, or iPhone users are shown a choice, even a few points of share moving away from Google would make Microsoft Advertising worth more attention than most UK advertisers currently give it.
- **Apple becomes an interesting player.** Apple loses a huge payment if the deal goes. It has the scale to build or partner on something of its own, and it announced its own AI features at WWDC in June.
- **Search ad prices could soften.** Real competition for advertisers' budgets should, in theory, put some pressure on cost per click. I'd treat that as the most speculative of the lot.
- **Measurement gets messier.** More engines with meaningful share means more platforms to track, more attribution arguments and more reporting work.

## What I'd do now

Very little, practically. If you're not already running Microsoft Advertising, it's worth importing your Google campaigns and running them at a modest budget anyway, partly because the traffic is often cheaper and partly so you have some history if the market does shift. Keep your SEO fundamentals sound across engines, not tuned to whatever Google rewarded last quarter.

In the UK, the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act passed in May, and the CMA will be able to give big platforms "strategic market status" with conduct rules attached. Google search is the obvious candidate. So the regulatory pressure on Google isn't only coming from Washington, and it may move faster here than an American appeal will.

I'll be reading the full opinion over the weekend. It runs to nearly 300 pages, and the sections on how Google sets ad prices are where I'm starting.
