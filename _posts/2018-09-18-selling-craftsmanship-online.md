A few months into looking after ecommerce at Authentic Bespoke, the problem I keep coming back to is persuading someone to pay a premium for something they can't touch.

In a shop like Budd's in Piccadilly Arcade, the answer takes care of itself. You pick up the shirt, feel the cloth, notice the collar, try it on, and talk to someone who has been doing this for years. Online, all of that has to be done with a screen, a few photographs and some words. Most of the difference in price comes from things that are hard to see in a photo: the cloth, how it's cut, how it's finished, how it will wear in five years.

So it's mostly an explaining problem, and I've been thinking a lot about how to do the explaining well.

## Photography carries most of the weight

For a product like this the photographs do far more work than the copy. I've come to think a decent set needs to answer a few questions without anyone reading a word:

- **What is the cloth actually like?** A close-up showing weave and texture tells you more than "fine two-fold cotton" ever will.
- **What are the details?** Collar shape, cuffs, buttons, stitching. These are where the money goes, so show them.
- **How does it look on a person?** Flat shots are honest about colour and shape, but people need to see the fit.
- **Is the colour right?** Returns caused by "it looked different on screen" are avoidable with careful lighting and a bit of discipline in editing.

The temptation with a heritage brand is to lead with atmosphere: oak panelling, old ledgers, moody lighting. There's a place for that on the brand pages. On a product page, I'd rather the customer could see the product.

## Copy that does a job

Product descriptions in this sector tend towards one of two failings. Either two lines of fairly generic text, or a paragraph of heritage that says nothing about the item itself.

What I want from a description is the information a good shop assistant would give you if you asked. What the cloth is and where it's from. How the fit compares to other shirts the customer might know. How to care for it. Why this detail is done this particular way. That's also, conveniently, the kind of copy that helps with search, because it uses the words people actually type when they're researching a purchase.

There's an SEO angle beyond that. As I wrote about the [Medic update](/medic-update-eat-ymyl-20180814/) last month, Google is increasingly interested in who is behind the content and whether they know what they're talking about. A business with a century of making things has real expertise. It just needs to get it onto the page.

## Telling the story without overdoing it

History is a genuine advantage. A business that has been making shirts since 1910 has something no start-up can buy. But I think the story works best in support of the product rather than instead of it. I don't think many people buy a shirt because of a founding date, but it does help them believe the shirt will be good.

The things that seem to land best are concrete and human: who makes the shirts, how long a particular process takes, what happens when you come in for a fitting. Anything that reads like a brochure gets skimmed, I suspect.

## Taking the risk out of buying

The final piece is reassurance. Someone spending this kind of money online, often for the first time with the brand, wants to know what happens if it doesn't fit. Clear sizing guidance, an easy returns process, and a way to ask a real person a question before buying all do a lot of work. I'd put these near the price, where the doubt actually arises, rather than in the footer.

None of this is complicated. Most of it is just taking the knowledge that already exists in the shop and putting it where an online customer can find it. It's slower than running a sale. I think it pays back better for this kind of brand, though I'll know more in a year.
