Non-essential shops in England reopened on 15 June, and the pictures of queues outside Primark were everywhere that morning. Ten days on, the question I keep getting asked, and keep asking myself, is how much of the online shopping that happened during lockdown will stay online.

The scale of the jump is not in doubt. The ONS figures put online at around a fifth of all UK retail sales before the pandemic. In May it was <a href="https://www.ons.gov.uk/businessindustryandtrade/retailindustry/bulletins/retailsales/may2020" target="_blank" rel="noopener">33.4%</a>, the highest on record, up from 30.8% in April. That's several years of normal growth squeezed into a couple of months, and it happened because people had very little choice.

So it'll fall back somewhat. The interesting question is how far, and for which kinds of purchase.

## Things I think will stay online

**Habits that were forced on people who'd never tried them.** A lot of older customers ordered online for the first time this spring, often with help from their children over the phone. Once you've set up an account, saved your card and had a parcel arrive without drama, the barrier has gone. Not all of those people will keep doing it, but a decent share will.

**Repeat purchases of things you already know.** If you know your shirt size in a particular brand, or you're replacing a wallet with the same one, there's not much the shop adds. I'd expect replenishment and repeat buying to stay online at a higher rate than before.

**Grocery, at least partly.** Supermarket delivery slots were the hardest thing in the country to get in late March. The capacity the supermarkets have added isn't going to disappear, and plenty of households have discovered they quite like not doing a big shop.

## Things I think will drift back

**First purchases of considered, expensive things.** For a first bespoke shirt, a leather bag you'll carry for fifteen years, or anything where fit and feel matter, many people still want to see it and touch it. Lockdown moved some of those purchases online because there was no alternative. I'd expect a lot of them to go back to shops once people are comfortable going in.

**Shopping as something to do.** Plenty of Saturday wanders round a town centre end without buying anything much, and I expect those to come back slowly as people feel safer.

**Gifts with a bit of theatre.** Being able to walk out of a shop with something wrapped is part of the point for a lot of gift buying.

## The awkward middle

The trouble is that shops aren't really back to normal. Fitting rooms are closed in many places, there are one-way systems and queues, and browsing feels awkward when there's a hand gel station at the door and a member of staff counting people in. For a luxury purchase where the experience is a big part of the value, a reduced shop experience may not beat a good website. Office workers are also mostly still at home, and the City and West End are very quiet, which matters a lot to shops that depend on that footfall.

My guess is that online's share settles well above where it was in February and well below where it was in May. If you're planning a budget, I'd plan on that, and I'd be wary of anyone who claims to know the number.

## What it means for heritage brands

For the kind of brands I work with at Authentic Bespoke, I think the lesson from the last three months is that the website has to carry more of the explaining that used to happen in a shop. That means better product information, clearer sizing advice, proper photography of details, and easy access to a real person who knows the product. Video calls with a shop team, which a lot of luxury brands tried during lockdown, are an interesting example. I'm not sure how many customers want that day to day, but for a first purchase it could be exactly the right bridge.

The other thing to protect is the new customer data. A lot of brands picked up customers who'd never bought direct before. Some of them will drift back to department stores or other channels now. The ones that keep them will be the ones who give them a reason to come back: good service, sensible follow-up emails, and not bombarding them with discounts that teach them to wait for the next one.

I'll come back to this in the autumn, when we've got a few months of reopened trading to look at.
