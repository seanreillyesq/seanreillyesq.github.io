A year after [the first helpful content update](/helpful-content-update-automation-20220830/), Google started rolling out the latest one on 14 September. It came barely a week after the August core update finished, so anyone trying to read their Search Console graphs right now has my sympathy.

What interests me more than the rollout is the wording. Google updated its guidance on creating helpful content alongside the update, and a few small edits say a lot.

## "Written by people" has gone

The old description of the helpful content system talked about rewarding content written by people, for people. That has been changed to helpful content created for people. The reference to who or what wrote it has been taken out.

That fits with what Google said in February, that it judges content on quality rather than how it was produced. But last year's update came with a pointed question about "extensive automation", and plenty of people took it as a warning against AI writing tools. This edit softens that considerably.

I don't read it as an invitation to publish machine output by the thousand. The rest of the guidance is still about experience, originality and whether a page adds something. My guess is that Google can't reliably tell who wrote a page, so it has stopped implying that it can.

## Third-party content on your domain

The second change is new guidance about content hosted on a site, or its subdomains, that is mostly independent of the main site's purpose and produced without much involvement from the site owner. Google says that kind of content can affect site-wide signals, and suggests blocking it from being indexed if it isn't helpful.

You can see what this is aimed at. Over the past couple of years, voucher code sections, product round-ups and casino guides have turned up on the domains of well-known publishers, ranking on the strength of the publisher's name rather than anything the publisher knows. Google seems to be saying it has noticed.

For a retailer, the equivalent risk is a blog or "guides" section handed to an outside content supplier and never really read by anyone inside the business. If that describes yours, it's worth a look.

## Faking freshness

Two smaller additions are about trying to look fresh: changing the date on a page without making meaningful changes, and adding or removing large amounts of content mainly in the hope of seeming more current.

The date trick is common. Plenty of "best of" pages update the year in the title every January and change nothing else. Google now names it outright as something that suggests search-first content.

Deleting content has also been a fashionable fix since last year's update. Some of that pruning is sensible. Some of it was done because a blog post said old content hurts you, and Google now seems to be warning against that.

## If you lost traffic

Google also added a note for sites that saw a drop after a helpful content update. Its advice is to self-assess against the guidance, and it accepts that some sites may not need to do anything at all, because other content may simply have been judged more helpful. Recovery, where it happens, can still take months, because the classifier keeps checking over time. That matches what I saw people describe after last year's update: sites that made changes quickly didn't bounce back quickly.

## What I'd take from it

For anyone using AI tools for drafting and editing, with someone who knows the subject shaping the result, this is mildly reassuring. Sites with a lot of outsourced content on their domain, or a habit of changing dates, have more to think about.

The update is due to finish towards the end of the month. As with August, I'd wait until it's complete before drawing conclusions from the data.
