Black Friday falls this week in the middle of England's second lockdown. Non-essential shops have been closed since 5 November and won't reopen until 2 December, which the government confirmed yesterday. So for this year's Black Friday weekend, in England at least, the high street is mostly shut and nearly everything happens online.

That's never happened before, and it makes this a strange one to plan for.

## Black Friday has become most of November

The most obvious change is that "Black Friday" has stretched even further than usual. Plenty of big retailers started their deals in early November, some before lockdown was even announced, and many are running them through to Cyber Monday and beyond. Partly that's a deliberate attempt to spread demand so warehouses and carriers aren't swamped in a single weekend. Partly it's because nobody wanted to be the retailer that waited.

For shoppers, it means there's less urgency around the day itself. For retailers, it means a longer period of noisy, expensive advertising. Paid search and social costs always rise around Black Friday. This year, with every retailer pushed online at once, I'd expect the competition for attention to be fiercer and to last longer.

## Delivery is the real constraint

With shops closed, almost every purchase this week has to be delivered. The carriers have been under pressure since the spring and are now handling Black Friday and early Christmas volumes at the same time. Some have been warning retailers about capacity limits, and delays are being reported already.

If I were advising any ecommerce team this week, my first point would have nothing to do with promotions. It would be to make sure the delivery promises on the site are honest and cautious. A customer who orders on Black Friday and gets the parcel in mid-December is unlikely to be grateful for the 20% off.

## The luxury dilemma, again

I wrote about the [Black Friday paradox for luxury brands](/black-friday-luxury-paradox-20151124/) a few years ago, and my view hasn't changed much. Heavy discounting does real damage to a brand whose value depends on not being cheap. It trains customers to wait for the sale, and once they've learned that it's very hard to undo.

This year adds some pressure on the other side. Many brands have had a hard year, have stock left over from a disrupted spring and summer, and need cash. Black Friday is the biggest single chance to turn stock into money. I understand why people are tempted.

My suggestion for heritage and luxury brands would be to find ways to take part that don't involve cutting prices on the core range:

- a gift with purchase, or free personalisation, rather than a percentage off;
- early access to Christmas gifting for existing customers;
- clearing genuinely seasonal or discontinued stock through a clearly separate sale section, not across the whole site;
- extended returns for Christmas gifts, which helps customers buying for other people.

None of these will generate the same spike as 30% off everything. They also won't do the same damage to January full-price sales.

## People are buying differently

The more interesting change might be what people are buying. The pattern from the first lockdown seems to be repeating: anything for the home, comfortable clothing, hobbies, food and drink, and gifts that can be sent directly to someone you can't see in person. Tailoring and formalwear are having a harder time, since there's not much reason to dress up for a video call.

Sending gifts direct to a recipient is going to be big this Christmas, with so many families unsure whether they'll be able to meet up. If your checkout doesn't make it easy to send to a different address with a gift note and no prices on the packing slip, that's worth fixing before December.

## After the weekend

Shops reopen on 2 December, and England moves into a tougher version of the tier system. I'd expect some shoppers to go back to stores for the last few weeks before Christmas, especially for gifts. But the restrictions and the general nervousness mean online will still carry much more of Christmas than usual, so I wouldn't treat this weekend as the peak.
