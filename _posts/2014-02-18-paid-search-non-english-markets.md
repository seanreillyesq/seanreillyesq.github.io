Most of what's written about paid search assumes two things: that everyone searches on Google, and that they do it in English. Step outside those and a lot of the received wisdom stops being useful. I've spent a good chunk of the last year running campaigns in markets where neither holds, and it has been a humbling education.

Start with the search engine itself. In Russia you are mostly not on Google, you are on Yandex, and it behaves differently enough that you can't just port your account across. China is Baidu, with its own rules and its own approvals process. Japan is the one that surprises people: Yahoo still commands an enormous share of search there, so a serious Japanese campaign means learning Yahoo's Sponsored Search as well as AdWords, and getting used to an interface that assumes you already read Japanese.

Then there's the language, which is where it gets genuinely hard.

You are choosing keywords and writing advert copy in a language you can't read. The obvious temptation is to reach for Google Translate and be done with it. Don't. Machine translation in its current state is fine for getting the gist of an incoming email; it is not fine for a two-line advert that has to be idiomatic, has to match how real people phrase a search, and has to fit a strict character count. I've seen back-translations that looked perfect and turned out to mean nothing to a native speaker, or worse, meant something faintly ridiculous. The tool has no idea what someone means, or that the word a real shopper types is rarely the dictionary one.

Arabic taught me the most. It's written right to left, which throws up rendering quirks before you've even thought about meaning. And a chunk of your audience won't type in Arabic script at all; they'll transliterate into Latin characters, so the "keyword" you need might be a phonetic spelling that no dictionary contains. There is no clever way around that from the outside. You need someone who lives in the language.

The answer to all of this is dull, and it's people. Native speakers, reviewing keywords and writing the copy, checking that a phrase lands the way you think it does. It costs money and it costs time, and it's tempting to skip because a machine will do a rough version for free. The rough version is the one that ends up in front of customers, saying something you didn't intend.

A few other things that catch you out, none of them about language:

- **Currency and value.** A cost-per-click that looks cheap converts into something quite different once you've done the maths, and the value of a customer varies market to market. You have to reset your sense of what "expensive" means for each one.
- **Local competitors you've never heard of.** The big global brands might be absent, and instead you're up against a domestic player who owns the market and the vocabulary around it.
- **Timing.** Weekends, working weeks and holidays don't line up. Scheduling a campaign to English office hours is an easy way to waste budget.

None of this is difficult in the way that a tricky bit of tracking is difficult. It's difficult because it resists the thing digital marketing is usually so good at, which is doing a lot from behind one desk in one language. You can't. The search box you're bidding into belongs to people whose world you barely know, and the only real fix is to pay someone who does.

So when a campaign crosses a language I can't read, the budget now starts with a native speaker's time and works outwards from there. Everything clever comes after that, assuming there's money left, which there often isn't.
