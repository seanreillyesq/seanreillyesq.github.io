Most people have a retailer or two they genuinely like, have bought from more than once, and have stopped reading entirely. The products are fine; the emails arrive most days, usually with a sale, and at some point the brain starts filing them alongside the junk.

Frequency works, in the narrow sense that more sends produce more revenue in the short run. Every additional email gets some clicks and some orders. The cost doesn't show up in the campaign report: the people who quietly stop opening, the ones who unsubscribe, and the slow training of your customers to wait for a discount.

For a mass-market retailer that cost might be worth paying. For a small luxury brand I don't think it is, and here's why.

## The list is small and valuable

A heritage brand's email list is usually modest in size, and a large part of its value sits with a relatively small number of loyal customers who buy several times a year. Those people already like the brand. They don't need persuading, and they certainly don't need a daily reminder that it exists.

What they want is to hear about things that matter to them. A new cloth, a restock of something that sold out, a seasonal collection, an event in the shop. If every email is worth opening, they keep opening. If half of them aren't, they start skimming, and eventually they miss the one that mattered.

## Discounts teach the wrong lesson

The fastest way to get a spike from an email list is a discount. It's also the fastest way to teach your best customers that full price is optional. Luxury brands depend on people being comfortable paying full price, because that's what funds the craftsmanship. A regular stream of percentage-off emails undermines that, and it's very hard to undo.

That doesn't mean never holding a sale. Most brands have end-of-season stock to clear. But I'd keep it to a predictable, limited moment, communicated plainly, and not the default subject line.

## What a good luxury email looks like

The emails I enjoy getting from brands in this world tend to share a few things:

- **One idea per email.** A single product, collection or story, not a grid of twelve items.
- **Something worth reading.** How a particular item is made, why a cloth behaves the way it does, how to look after leather. The kind of thing a good shop assistant would tell you.
- **Proper photography,** sized sensibly so the email loads quickly on a phone.
- **A plain-text feel where it suits.** A short note that reads like it came from a person can do better than a heavily designed template, especially for regular customers.
- **A clear way to talk to someone.** Reply-to that goes to a real inbox, not a no-reply address.

## Segmentation without overthinking it

You don't need a sophisticated CRM to do a much better job than the default. Three simple splits get you most of the way:

1. People who have bought, and people who haven't.
2. People who buy a particular category (shirts, or bags, or accessories).
3. People who have bought recently, and people who haven't bought in a long time.

A customer who has bought five shirts doesn't need the same welcome-style email as someone who signed up for a newsletter last week. Someone who hasn't bought in eighteen months might need a different kind of nudge, or might simply be better off not being emailed so often.

## Measure the right things

Open rates and click rates are fine as signals, but they reward frequency in a way that's misleading. I'd pay more attention to:

- Unsubscribes and spam complaints per send, tracked over time.
- The proportion of the list that has opened anything in the last six months.
- Revenue per subscriber over a quarter, not revenue per campaign.

If sending more emails increases revenue per campaign while the engaged share of the list shrinks, next year's numbers will pay for it. That's the pattern I'd most want to catch early.

None of this is new, but when a sales target is due the quickest option is always another send, and I understand why people take it.
