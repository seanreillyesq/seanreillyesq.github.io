Google started rolling out the August 2023 core update last Tuesday, the 22nd, and says it will take around two weeks to complete. It's the first core update since March. As usual there's very little detail on what has changed, and as usual the rank-tracking tools have been showing plenty of movement.

When traffic drops, the temptation is to start changing things straight away. So rather than speculate about what this update targets, here's the order I'd go through things in if you think it's hit you.

## 1. Wait for it to finish

I know. Nobody wants to hear that. But rankings bounce around during a rollout, sometimes in both directions, and a page that drops on day three can recover by day ten. Google's status dashboard will mark the update as complete. Until then, note what you're seeing and hold off on changes.

## 2. Confirm it's actually search

Before blaming Google, check that the drop is in organic search and not somewhere else. Look in Search Console, not only GA4, because GA4 is new enough for most sites that a tracking problem is just as likely an explanation. Things that can look like an algorithm hit:

- A tracking change, consent banner update or tag manager edit around the same date
- A site release that changed URLs, templates, internal links or robots rules
- Seasonality. Late August is a quiet fortnight for a lot of sectors in the UK, with schools off and half the country on holiday. Compare with the same weeks last year, not with July.

## 3. Find the shape of the drop

In Search Console, compare the two weeks after the 22nd with the two weeks before, then work out where the loss is concentrated:

- **By page type.** Category pages, product pages, blog posts, guides. Core updates often hit one type of content and leave others alone.
- **By query.** Branded or non-brand? Informational or commercial? A loss on "how to" queries means something different from a loss on product terms.
- **By position.** Falling from 3 to 6 on your important terms is a bigger problem than falling from 15 to 25 on a long tail of terms nobody clicked anyway.

Write this down, even roughly. You'll want it later when deciding what, if anything, to change.

## 4. Look at who replaced you

Search the queries you lost and see what ranks now. This is the most useful step and the one most often skipped. If you've been replaced by bigger brands, forums, manufacturer sites or pages with more depth, that tells you how Google has reassessed the query. Sometimes the honest conclusion is that their page is better than yours for what the searcher wants.

## 5. Read Google's questions, honestly

Google's guidance on core updates points to a list of self-assessment questions about content quality, expertise and whether the page gives real value compared with others in the results. They're easy to skim and say "yes, that's us". Try answering them as a sceptical customer would.

## What not to do

- **Don't delete large amounts of content in a hurry.** If something needs pruning, do it thoughtfully, not because a graph went down this week.
- **Don't buy links or start a "recovery" package** from whoever emails you first.
- **Don't rewrite every page.** You can't learn anything from changing everything at once.
- **Don't expect a quick bounce back.** Google has said that improvements may take time to show, and sometimes not until a later core update.

Google has also said a core update can move you down without you having done anything wrong; other pages may simply have been judged more relevant. That isn't much comfort if the drop costs you sales.
