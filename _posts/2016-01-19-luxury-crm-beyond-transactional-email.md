January is when everyone's inbox fills up with sale emails, and it's also when I end up thinking hardest about what our own email programme is actually for. Most of the CRM thinking I read is built around a fast-moving retailer. Someone buys a jumper, you send them a receipt, a "you might also like", a reminder in three weeks, a discount on their birthday. The whole machine assumes the next purchase is a few weeks away.

At Watchfinder that assumption is wrong. Someone who buys a watch from us might not buy another for three years. Some never will, and that's fine; a good watch is supposed to last. So a programme designed to squeeze out the next order in thirty days mostly ends up irritating people who have no intention of buying again this month.

## What changes when the purchase cycle is years long

The first thing that changes is what you count as success. Open rates and click rates on a weekly newsletter tell you something, but they don't tell you whether someone still thinks of you when they're ready to buy or sell again in 2018. That's the outcome that matters and it's hard to measure inside one campaign report.

The second is what you send. With a long cycle, the email has to earn its place on its own, without leaning on a promotion. If the only reason I'm writing to you is to show you stock, you'll tune out by the third email. If I'm writing to tell you something you'd want to know anyway, about a model you own or a reference you've looked at, you'll open the fourth one too.

A few things I'm convinced of at this point:

- **The customer after the sale matters more than the customer before it.** The weeks after someone buys an expensive watch are when they're most engaged and most nervous. Care advice, servicing information, what the warranty actually covers. This is the email they open, and most programmes go quiet right at that moment.
- **Owners are a different audience from browsers.** Someone who owns a particular model wants to hear about that model, its history, what it's doing in the market. Sending them the same newsletter as a first-time visitor wastes the one thing you know about them.
- **Frequency should follow interest.** Somebody who has been on the site four times this week looking at the same reference is in a very different place from somebody who bought in 2013. Treating them to the same Thursday send is lazy.
- **Discount-led email trains the wrong behaviour.** If every message carries an offer, people learn to wait for the offer. In luxury that's the last thing you want them learning.

## The bit I'm still working out

The obvious answer to all of this is personalisation, and I'm sold on the principle. The practice is harder. Our data on what someone owns, what they've looked at and what they've sold to us doesn't all live in one place, and stitching it together so an email can use it is more of a plumbing problem than a creative one.

I've been looking at tools like <a href="https://movableink.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Movable Ink</a>, which builds the content of an email at the moment it's opened rather than when it's sent. So the stock shown is whatever is available at that moment, rather than whatever was available on Tuesday when the campaign went out. For a business where individual pieces sell and disappear, that matters; clicking a watch in an email and landing on "sold" is a miserable experience.

I'm cautious about it too. Any tool like this is only as good as the data you feed it, and it's easy to buy the shiny layer on top before sorting the foundations underneath. I'd rather get three well-segmented emails going to the right people before we spend money on dynamic templates.

## Where I've landed for now

The test I've started applying to every email is whether the person receiving it would be glad they got it. Revenue this week matters too, obviously. But if the honest answer is no, I'd rather it didn't go.

That makes the programme slower to show results, and I'll have to defend it when the weekly numbers look flat. Next job on the list is getting ownership data and browsing data into the same place, because none of the above works properly until that's done.
