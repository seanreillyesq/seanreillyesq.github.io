When people talk about how customers find a retailer, the conversation usually starts and ends with Google. Rank for the right terms, bid on the right keywords, and you're in front of people at the moment they want to buy. For a lot of products that's a fair description of reality.

For watches it's maybe the last ten per cent of the story. By the time someone types a specific reference number into Google, they've usually already decided what they want, roughly what they should pay, and quite often who they're going to buy it from. The decisions that matter were made somewhere else, over weeks, sometimes years.

Here's where I see that happening.

## Forums

The watch world has some of the oldest and most active enthusiast forums on the internet. <a href="https://forums.watchuseek.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">WatchUSeek</a>, TimeZone, PuristSPro, the Rolex Forums, and plenty of brand-specific ones. Most of them look dated, which nobody there seems to mind. The people posting there know more about some of these watches than most salespeople do, and a thread asking "is this a fair price?" or "has anyone bought from X?" can shape hundreds of purchases without the retailer ever knowing it exists.

Forums don't respond well to marketing, and turning up to promote yourself usually goes badly. What seems to work is behaving well over a long period, so people say good things about you in threads you never see. Pricing transparency, honest condition descriptions and sorting problems out quickly all end up quoted back on forums, good and bad.

## YouTube

YouTube is where people go to see a watch on a wrist before they've seen one in a shop. Size, how the dial catches the light, what the bracelet looks like in motion. Photos don't do that, and most people buying a watch at this price have never handled the one they're considering.

Watchfinder has put a lot into its YouTube channel, and last year we [cut half the videos from it]({% post_url 2016-05-13-deleted-half-our-youtube-videos %}) to focus on what was actually useful. The main thing I've taken from it is that most of the value comes well before the purchase. Someone watches a review, then another, then a comparison, and months later they buy. Last-click analytics gives that sale to a brand search or a Shopping ad, and the video gets no credit at all.

## Instagram

Instagram has become the place where watch enthusiasts show off, and it's a huge one. Wrist shots, collection shots, new releases from Basel and SIHH within minutes of them being shown. For discovery, it's where people come across a model they didn't know they wanted. It's less good at getting anyone to click through and buy, and I'd be suspicious of anyone who tells you otherwise right now, but as a place where desire gets started it's hard to ignore.

## Editorial sites and magazines

<a href="https://www.hodinkee.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Hodinkee</a> in particular, plus the print magazines that still matter in this category. These set the tone for which watches are "in" and which are overlooked, and that filters straight down into what people ask for.

## The people around them

This one is the hardest to measure and probably the most important. Watch buyers talk to each other. A friend's recommendation, a collector they trust, a dealer they've used before. Dark social, the sharing that happens in WhatsApp groups and private messages, shows up in analytics as direct traffic and nothing else.

## What this means for where the money goes

If most of the research happens away from search, then judging every channel on the last click before purchase is going to make search look like a genius and everything else look like a cost. It's the same problem I keep running into whenever anyone asks what our content is actually worth.

What I try to do, imperfectly:

- Treat YouTube and editorial as demand creation, and judge them on things like brand search and returning visitors rather than direct sales.
- Make sure the basics are flawless where the research ends up. If someone has spent three months deciding, the product page, the price and the phone number need to be right when they arrive.
- Read the forums. Not to post, mostly, but because nowhere else will tell you so plainly what customers think of you and your competitors.

I don't think anyone in this sector has properly cracked how to measure the whole journey. We certainly haven't. For now, brand search, returning visitors and a regular read of the forums are the best rough guide I have.
