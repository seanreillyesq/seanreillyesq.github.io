Before the new guesses, the old ones. Last December I wrote [six predictions for 2019](/2019-predictions-core-updates-20181218/), and promised to check back.

## How 2019 went

- **Core updates as routine.** Right. Three confirmed ones, in [March](/march-2019-core-update-20190320/), [June](/june-2019-core-update-site-diversity-20190611/) and [September](/september-2019-core-update-nofollow-changes-20190930/), with Google pre-announcing the last two. I didn't see the pre-announcements coming.
- **Mobile-first indexing for most of the web.** Broadly right. Google made it the default for new sites from July, and says more than half of the sites in its results are already indexed that way.
- **Speed creeping up the agenda.** Fair. Search Console has started testing a speed report, and Chrome has talked about flagging slow sites to users.
- **The first big GDPR fine.** Right, and quickly. In January France's regulator fined Google €50 million (about £44 million) over consent for ad personalisation. A household name, and about consent rather than a breach, which is what I'd guessed. The ICO followed in July with notices of intent to fine British Airways and Marriott.
- **Voice search stays overhyped.** I'm calling that one right too, though I accept it's hard to prove a negative.
- **Instagram becomes a proper shop.** Partly. Checkout inside Instagram launched in the US in March with a small group of brands. It hasn't reached the UK.

And one I didn't predict at all: [BERT](/google-bert-search-update-20191029/), which arrived in October and was extended to more than 70 languages earlier this month.

## 2020: privacy gets real

This year's predictions mostly have one theme. For a few years privacy has mostly been treated as a compliance job for the legal team. I think 2020 is when it starts to change how marketing actually works.

### 1. CCPA makes US privacy everyone's problem

The California Consumer Privacy Act takes effect on 1 January. It applies to businesses above certain thresholds that handle California residents' data, which will include a fair number of UK businesses selling into the US. Most small UK retailers won't hit the thresholds, but I expect large platforms to apply CCPA-style controls broadly, and "Do Not Sell My Personal Information" links to start appearing on plenty of sites, including some that don't strictly need them.

### 2. Browsers keep squeezing third-party cookies

Safari's Intelligent Tracking Prevention has been tightened several times this year, and Firefox now blocks known tracking cookies by default. Chrome is the one that matters most by share, and in August Google outlined what it calls a Privacy Sandbox, a set of proposals for advertising without third-party cookies. My guess is Google announces a timeline for limiting third-party cookies in Chrome during 2020, even if the actual change is a couple of years off. Anyone whose retargeting or attribution depends on third-party cookies should plan on that basis.

### 3. Cookie banners get stricter in the UK and EU

The ICO's cookie guidance in July and the European court's Planet49 ruling in October both point the same way: pre-ticked boxes don't count as consent, and non-essential cookies, including analytics, need consent before they're set. Most banners I see still don't do this properly. I expect enforcement to start, probably against a few large sites first, and banners to become much more visible as a result.

### 4. Measurement gets worse before it gets better

Put the first three together and the result is less data. Fewer visitors tracked, shorter cookie lifetimes, more gaps in attribution. I think 2020 is the year a lot of marketing teams notice their analytics numbers drifting away from what the tills say, and have to explain why. The useful response is to lean on first-party data, your own customer records and email list, and to get comfortable with modelled numbers rather than pretending every conversion can be traced.

### 5. Brexit settles into the background, slowly

After last week's election, the UK is now very likely to leave the EU at the end of January. For data protection, GDPR has been written into UK law, so day-to-day compliance shouldn't change much. The open question is whether the EU will recognise UK data protection as adequate, which affects data flowing from Europe. That probably won't be resolved quickly.

## For smaller brands

None of this is bad news for small heritage brands. Businesses that rely on a loyal customer list and a good email programme are less dependent on third-party tracking than those built on paid social and retargeting. If anything, I think 2020 could suit them better than it suits the big spenders on retargeting.
