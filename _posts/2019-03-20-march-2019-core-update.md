In December I guessed that broad core updates would become the normal rhythm of 2019. It took Google until the second week of March to prove the point.

On 12 March Google confirmed that a broad core update was rolling out. Within a day the SEO world had started calling it Florida 2, after the notorious update of 2003, and Google responded by asking everyone to call it the "March 2019 Core Update" instead. That's a small thing, but it tells you something about how Google wants these updates understood. A nickname suggests a target, something that was punished, whereas a name with a date on it suggests routine maintenance, and Google clearly wants the latter.

## What seems to have moved

It's only a week in, and early analysis of any update is half data and half people looking for patterns that confirm what they already thought. With that caveat, a few things keep coming up:

- **Some of the sites hit by last August's Medic update have recovered**, at least partly. Health and finance sites are again among the biggest movers, in both directions.
- **Brand and trust signals look important again.** The sites gaining tend to be the ones with obvious authors, clear ownership and a reputation outside their own website.
- **There's no single on-page factor anyone can point to.** No sign that it's about links, or speed, or a particular type of content.

That last point is the useful one. If the winners and losers don't share a technical trait, the change is about how Google judges overall quality, which is what Google keeps saying.

## E-A-T hasn't gone away

When I wrote about [the Medic update](/medic-update-eat-ymyl-20180814/) last August, E-A-T (expertise, authoritativeness and trustworthiness) had only just escaped from the quality rater guidelines into everyday SEO conversation. Seven months on it's everywhere, sometimes usefully and sometimes as a magic phrase that gets attached to any recommendation to make it sound important.

I still think the most practical reading of it is fairly simple. Google wants to rank pages written by people who know what they're talking about, published by organisations people trust, on sites that don't hide who they are. Most of what follows from that is ordinary good practice: author information, an about page that says who is behind the business, contact details, reviews from somewhere other than your own site, and content that is accurate and kept up to date.

For an ecommerce site that last part is easy to neglect. Product pages that were written once and never touched, care guides that contradict each other, delivery information that's two years out of date. None of that will get you penalised as such, but it's exactly the sort of thing a rater would mark down.

## What I'd do this week

Probably nothing dramatic. If your traffic dropped, the first step is to check whether it really was the update and not seasonality, a tracking problem or a change on your own site. Compare like-for-like weeks and look at Search Console rather than just analytics.

If it was the update, Google's guidance hasn't changed. There's no fix to apply, and recoveries tend to come with later core updates rather than in the days after. That's frustrating to hear, particularly if you report to someone who wants an answer by Friday. But I'd rather say "we'll spend the next few months making the site better and expect to see it at the next update" than make sweeping changes to pages that might not have been the problem.

If your traffic went up, the same advice applies in reverse: don't assume you've done something clever, and keep doing the work.

## Explaining it upwards

Back to the naming, because it helps if you have to explain this to a board. "Florida 2" sounds like a penalty, and a penalty sounds like somebody did something wrong. For most sites that's not what happened; Google reassesses rankings several times a year now, and this was one of those times. I'd use Google's name in the report. I expect we'll be having the same conversation again by the summer.
