September has been a busy month for anyone who watches Google closely. A core update started on 24 September, pre-announced like [the June one](/june-2019-core-update-site-diversity-20190611/). And two weeks before that, Google made the bigger change of the month, though it got less attention: it rewrote the rules on nofollow links.

## The September 2019 Core Update

The third confirmed core update of the year, and by now the pattern is familiar. Search Liaison announced it, the standard guidance followed, and the SEO forums filled up with screenshots of traffic charts.

Early signs are that it's milder than June, at least in the sectors I keep an eye on. Some sites that dropped in June have recovered a bit; others have dropped again. That's consistent with what Google has said all along about core updates: they reassess sites broadly, and a site that lost out in one round can gain in the next if things improve, or if Google's view of what's relevant shifts.

I'd treat it the same way as the earlier ones this year. Check the dates against your own traffic, rule out the boring explanations, and resist rewriting half the site in a panic. If you've had three updates in a row going against you, it's probably time for a hard look at content quality and trust signals rather than another wait-and-see.

## The nofollow change

On 10 September Google announced two new link attributes and a change to how it treats the old one:

- **rel="sponsored"** for links that are paid for, advertising, or part of a sponsorship.
- **rel="ugc"** for links in user-generated content such as comments and forum posts.
- **rel="nofollow"** stays, but Google will now treat it, and the two new attributes, as a *hint* rather than an instruction. For ranking, that applies straight away. For crawling and indexing, Google says it will start treating nofollow as a hint from March next year.

Nofollow was introduced in 2005 to fight comment spam, and over the years it got stretched to cover everything from paid links to links site owners just didn't want to vouch for. Google's argument is that by ignoring all those links, it's been throwing away useful information about the web. Treating them as hints lets it use some of that information when it thinks it should.

## What I'd actually do

For most ecommerce sites, the practical answer is: not very much, and not in a hurry.

- **You don't need to change existing nofollow links.** Google has said nofollow remains acceptable for sponsored links, and there's no requirement to go back and relabel anything.
- **For new paid placements, use rel="sponsored".** If you work with bloggers, gift products to Instagram accounts that link back, or run affiliate links, sponsored is now the clearest way to mark them. Nofollow still works for this.
- **If your site has reviews, comments or a community area**, rel="ugc" is the honest label for links people add there. Most platforms and review apps will need to update their output before this is straightforward, so it's worth checking with whoever supplies yours.

The thing I'd watch is the "hint" part. Some people will read it as a sign that nofollow links now pass value, and will start chasing them again. Maybe some will, for Google's own purposes, in some cases. But Google isn't saying which, and I wouldn't build a link strategy around an unpredictable maybe. I'd carry on as before and concentrate on links from people who mention you because they want to.

## Two kinds of change

September had one of each. The core update is the type of change Google now makes several times a year, broad and unexplained. The nofollow announcement was specific, documented and came with a timeline. I much prefer that sort, though it's the core updates that show up in the traffic reports.

So the September jobs list is short: check the update dates against your own data, and tell whoever manages your partnerships about rel="sponsored".
