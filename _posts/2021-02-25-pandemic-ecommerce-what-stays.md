On Monday the Prime Minister set out the roadmap for leaving lockdown, and non-essential shops in England are pencilled in to reopen no earlier than 12 April. It's almost a year since the first lockdown, and for the first time in a while people around me are talking about what happens after rather than how to get through the next month.

So this feels like the right moment to try to separate the changes that will stick from the ones that were simply the only option available. I've been working in luxury ecommerce at Authentic Bespoke throughout, and my view is shaped by that. A heritage leather goods or shirtmaking customer behaves differently from someone buying a home gym in a panic, and I'll try to be clear about which is which.

## What I think stays

**Older customers who learned to buy online.** This is the one I'm most confident about. A lot of people who had never bought anything significant online did so in the last year because they had no choice. They set up accounts, saved card details, worked out how returns worked. Having done it once, I can't see many of them going back. For brands whose customers skew older, which is many heritage brands, this is the biggest change of the lot.

**Expectations of service online.** With the shops shut, customers have got used to asking questions by email, chat or phone and getting a proper answer from someone who knows the product. Once you've had that, a generic contact form with a five-day reply time feels like a slight. I expect that standard to hold even when the doors are open again.

**Click and collect, and local delivery options.** Retailers who built these out of necessity have found that a fair few customers like them. They'll stay because they're cheap to keep running once they exist.

**The website as the first visit.** Even when someone does go into a shop, they'll have looked first. More of them than before will arrive having already chosen, and use the visit to check the colour or the fit.

## What I think fades

**Category booms.** Home fitness, home office furniture, baking equipment, garden furniture. Some of that demand was pulled forward from future years and will leave a hole behind it. I'd be cautious about anyone planning 2022 budgets on the back of 2020 numbers in those categories.

**Tolerance for slow delivery.** Customers were forgiving last spring because everyone was in the same position. That patience is already wearing thin and I don't think it survives the reopening.

**The idea that luxury can be done entirely online.** Here I'll disagree with some of the louder voices. Online share of retail has jumped (the ONS put it at 35.2% of all retail sales in January), and plenty of people are calling that a permanent reset. For everyday categories, maybe. For high-value, tactile products I don't believe it. People want to feel the weight of a bag, try a shirt collar, talk to someone who knows how the thing was made. They've been denied that for a year, and I expect a real appetite for it once it's allowed.

## What I'd do with that

If I'm right, the job for the next year is to keep the gains without assuming the online share stays where it is.

- Keep investing in product information. Photography, measurements, materials, care. The customer who can't touch the product needs more from the page, and so will the one who's checking before a shop visit.
- Keep the people who answered customer questions during lockdown doing it, and make it easy to reach them from the site.
- Measure online's role in sales that complete in person. That's hard and I haven't got it right yet, but if a shop reopens and online revenue drops, the lazy reading will be that online "stopped working".
- Don't let the reopening become an excuse to cut the ecommerce team back to pre-pandemic size. Some of the traffic will move back to shops, but I'd expect most of the research to stay online.

I'm conscious this is a guess written before anything has actually reopened. Ask me again in the autumn.
