Christmas planning normally starts in the summer and is mostly settled by now. This year I've rewritten the plan more times than I'd like to admit, and I suspect most ecommerce teams are in the same position.

The rule of six came in yesterday. Cases are rising again, there's open talk of a second wave, and nobody can say with confidence whether shops will be open in December. On top of that, the Brexit transition period ends on 31 December, right in the middle of the busiest selling weeks for anyone shipping to Europe. So this is my attempt to think through what can be planned and what can't.

## Assume more of Christmas happens online

Whatever happens with restrictions, I think the safe assumption is that more of Christmas shopping happens online this year than last, possibly a lot more. Even if shops stay open, plenty of people won't want to queue or shop in crowds. If shops close again, online gets everything.

That changes the shape of the season as well as the size. Pressure on carriers was already intense in the spring, and every retailer is now planning for the same thing at the same time. My expectation is that the last safe posting dates will matter more than usual, and that the week before Christmas will be much less reliable for delivery than normal.

## Pull demand earlier

The obvious response is to encourage people to shop earlier. The less obvious bit is doing it without just starting the discounting earlier.

For heritage and luxury brands, I'd rather give people practical reasons to buy in October and November:

- clear and honest delivery cut-offs on the site from early on, stated more cautiously than in a normal year;
- gift guides and gift services ready by October rather than late November;
- making personalisation and monogramming cut-offs very visible, since those take longer;
- email content about gifting that starts earlier and is more useful, not just more frequent.

Amazon has pushed Prime Day into the autumn this year, which will pull some general retail spending earlier too. I wouldn't compete with it directly, but it's worth knowing when the market will be noisy.

## Stock is a bet either way

Inventory is the hardest part. Order too much and you're stuck with it in January. Order too little and you miss the one season this year that might actually go well. Lead times from workshops and suppliers are still unpredictable, and anything made abroad has shipping uncertainty on top.

My own approach is to go deep on the core products that sell every year and that customers come back for, and lighter on seasonal lines. A classic shirt or a bag that's been in the range for years will usually still sell in February if it doesn't go in December, which is not true of most seasonal lines.

## Europe and 31 December

For any UK brand with European customers, the end of the transition period is a real planning issue, and it's getting lost under the COVID news. From 1 January, parcels to EU customers become exports with customs declarations, and there are open questions about duties and VAT that may mean the customer paying extra charges on delivery. Nobody wants a customer's Christmas present held at customs, or a January order arriving with an unexpected bill.

I'd be setting a clear cut-off for EU deliveries well before Christmas and telling those customers about it early, and starting the conversation with carriers now about how they'll handle shipments after 1 January.

## Keep the plan flexible

The biggest change in my thinking this year is that the peak plan can't be a single fixed thing. I'm trying to build it as a set of scenarios: shops open as now, shops closed again in parts of the country, and a full national lockdown. For each, the questions are the same: what do we promote, how much do we spend, what do we promise on delivery, and how do we staff customer service.

It feels like a lot of work for scenarios that won't all happen. But after the spring I'd rather have the decisions half-made in advance than make them in a hurry in November.

I've written about [Black Friday and luxury](/black-friday-luxury-paradox-20151124/) before, and I don't think this year changes my view on heavy discounting. If anything, with demand moving online anyway, the case for joining the race to the bottom looks weaker than usual.
