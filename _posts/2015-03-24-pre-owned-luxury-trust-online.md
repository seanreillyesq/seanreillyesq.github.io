Buying a new product from a big retailer online asks for very little trust. The item is identical to the thousands of others they sell, the brand stands behind it, and if it's wrong you send it back. Buying a second-hand luxury item online asks for a great deal more. The item is unique, its condition is a matter of opinion, fakes exist and are getting better, and the amount of money involved means a mistake actually hurts.

Pre-owned luxury online has been growing for a while now, across watches, handbags, jewellery and designer clothing, and the businesses doing it well are essentially in the trust business first. Looking at how the better ones present themselves, a few things come up again and again.

## Show the actual item

The biggest difference between new and pre-owned is that the buyer wants to see the item they'd actually receive. Every mark, every scuff, the clasp, the serial number where that's appropriate, the inside of the bag. A stock photo on a pre-owned listing is a red flag to anyone who's done this before.

It costs money. Proper photography of every individual item doesn't scale the way a catalogue does, and for a business turning over a lot of stock it's a real operational overhead. I don't see a way round it, though; listings that skimp on photos look like they've got something to hide, even when they haven't.

## Be specific about condition

"Excellent condition" means nothing; every seller says it. What reassures is a grading system that's explained somewhere on the site, applied consistently, and backed up by the photographs. If you say a watch has light wear on the bracelet, the picture should show light wear on the bracelet, because buyers do check.

## Paperwork and provenance

In watches the phrase is "box and papers", and its presence or absence changes the price. More widely, anything that tells the story of where an item has been helps: original receipts, service records, certificates, the year of purchase. Where an item doesn't have them, saying so plainly is better than leaving the buyer to wonder. If they're missing and the listing says nothing, most buyers will assume the worst.

## A guarantee with real terms

Most pre-owned sellers offer some kind of authenticity guarantee and a warranty. The ones that work are specific: how long, what's covered, what happens if something goes wrong, who pays to send it back. A vague promise in the footer doesn't carry much weight. A clear, generous returns policy also tells the customer you're confident enough in the item to let them change their mind.

## Prove you're a real business

This sounds basic, but online it matters more than it should. A physical address, ideally somewhere you can visit. A phone number answered by a person. Company registration details. Independent reviews on a site you don't control. Press coverage, if you've had any. The buyer is asking themselves whether this business will still be here in a year if something goes wrong, and all of these help answer it.

## Where marketing fits

None of this is glamorous, and very little of it looks like marketing in the advertising sense. But in a category like this I think the marketing team's most valuable work is often making sure all of these trust signals are present, consistent and easy to find at the moment the customer is hesitating. That usually means the product page and the checkout, not a campaign.

Advertising still has a job to do in getting people to the site. But whether someone then hands over several thousand pounds for a second-hand item depends much more on what they find when they get there.
