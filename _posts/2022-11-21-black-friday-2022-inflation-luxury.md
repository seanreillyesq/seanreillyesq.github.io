Black Friday is on Friday, and this is the strangest run-up to it I can remember. Last Wednesday the ONS put October's inflation at 11.1%, the highest in about 41 years. Energy bills have gone up, mortgages have gone up, and my own inbox is filling with discount codes that started a fortnight early. On top of that, the CWU has called Royal Mail strikes for Thursday and Friday this week, then again on 30 November and 1 December.

For premium and luxury brands the usual Black Friday question is "do we take part at all?" This year it has a sharper edge.

## Two pressures pulling opposite ways

The first is obvious. Customers feel poorer, even many who aren't, and a discount gives them permission to buy. If you sit out entirely, you may watch people who'd normally buy from you spend their reduced budget on somebody who did offer 20% off.

The second is that discounting is very hard to undo. A brand that marks everything down this week teaches its customers to wait for the next sale. For a business built on full price and craft, that's hard to reverse. And with costs rising for the business as well (materials, energy, shipping) margins are already thinner than last year. A blanket 25% off on top of that can turn a busy week into a loss-making one.

## What I'd consider instead of a sitewide discount

There's a middle ground, and most of it has been around for years:

- **Gift with purchase.** Adds value without changing the price on the product. Works best when the gift is something people want, not old stock.
- **A threshold offer.** Something off orders above a certain value. It protects the lower end of the range and nudges basket size up rather than down.
- **Early access for existing customers.** Rewards loyalty, keeps the offer out of the public eye, and doesn't train the whole market.
- **Limited, named lines.** Discount specific older styles or end-of-season items, and say so plainly, rather than marking down the core range.
- **Free or faster delivery.** This year especially, with the post in doubt, delivery certainty may be worth more to a customer than 10% off.

## The delivery problem

The strikes deserve their own section, because I think they're being underplayed. If Royal Mail is the main carrier and the strike dates hold, Black Friday orders could sit in a depot through the weekend and into the second pair of strike days. Customers who bought gifts and then wait ten days will mostly blame the shop they bought from.

Things I'd do this week if I hadn't already:

1. Check which carrier each delivery option uses and whether there's a courier alternative for the strike days.
2. Put honest dispatch and delivery messaging on product pages and in the basket, not buried in a delivery FAQ.
3. Brief customer service with a holding line for "where's my order?" before the emails arrive.
4. Think about whether next-day promises should come off the site for the strike days.

None of it takes long.

## Reading the results honestly

When it's over, the temptation will be to compare Black Friday revenue with last year and declare success or failure. I'd add margin to that comparison, and the share of revenue that came from people who'd bought at full price in the last six months. If a large chunk of the week's sales went to existing customers at a discount who would have bought anyway, that's margin handed back for nothing. Inflation makes that more pointed this year.

I'll be interested to see what the payment providers' figures say about spend versus volume once they're out. My guess is lower volume and slightly higher order values, but it's only a guess.
