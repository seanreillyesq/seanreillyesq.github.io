Two years ago I wrote about [why Black Friday is awkward for luxury brands]({% post_url 2015-11-24-black-friday-luxury-paradox %}), where discounting can do real damage to how people see the brand. This year I'm on the other side of the fence. In mass-market retail, opting out isn't really on the table; customers expect deals, competitors will run them, and the only question is how well you handle it.

Black Friday is on 24 November, with Cyber Monday on the 27th. Here's what I think needs to be done by now, or very soon. Most of it is operations rather than marketing.

## Load

The thing I worry about most is the site falling over at 8am on Friday, just as the email goes out to the whole list.

- Load test against realistic traffic, ideally a multiple of last year's peak hour, not your normal day. Include the checkout and search, which are usually where things break first, not just the homepage.
- Know what happens when it does slow down. Is there a queue page? Who gets the call, and are they available?
- Freeze changes. No new features, extensions or theme updates from a week or so before until after Cyber Monday.
- Check the full-page cache is actually working, and that a sale banner or a promotion rule doesn't quietly switch it off for every page.

## Stock

Selling things you don't have means a month of cancellations and refunds afterwards. Make sure stock levels on the site update often enough to cope with the pace of sales, especially on the products you're promoting. If your stock sync runs overnight, that's not going to be enough this week.

It's also worth being honest about which products you're discounting. For a lot of retailers Black Friday is a good chance to clear older stock, and in cycling that fits neatly with the model-year changeover I [wrote about in September]({% post_url 2017-09-19-cycle-retail-market-2017 %}). Leading with last season's lines, rather than taking the margin out of current best sellers, can make the whole weekend more profitable.

## Promotions and pricing

Test every promotion rule, discount code and price change before it goes live, on the live site if you can do it safely. Check that discounts don't stack in ways nobody intended. A code meant for one category that applies to everything, combined with a sitewide discount, is the sort of mistake that ends up on deal forums within an hour.

Make sure prices are consistent everywhere: the website, the Google Shopping feed, marketplaces, and any email or social posts. A price in an ad that doesn't match the site gets products disapproved and annoys customers.

## Delivery

Check with your couriers what they can actually collect and deliver in the week after Black Friday, and set expectations on the site accordingly. Published delivery times that turn out to be fiction create a wave of "where's my order" contacts just when you can least handle them. Bulky items like bikes are harder still, and if anything needs building or checking before dispatch, that becomes the bottleneck.

## Customer service

Customer service volume goes up with sales, and then goes up again a week later when deliveries are due. Plan staffing for both peaks. Prepare answers to the obvious questions in advance: delivery times, returns, price matching, whether the deal applies to something that was bought the week before.

## Then the marketing

With all that sorted, the marketing part is fairly straightforward:

- Tell your existing customers first. Email is still the channel that does the most work over this weekend.
- Raise budgets and loosen limits on paid search and Shopping for the key days, and watch them closely, because costs go up for everyone.
- Use early access or a teaser in the week before, so people know to come to you rather than starting their search on someone else's site.
- Have a plan for the Monday and the week after. The whole thing has turned from a day into a week or more, and shoppers have learned that.

## And afterwards

Write down what went wrong while you still remember, and keep it somewhere you'll find it next October.

Black Friday isn't going away, and I'm not sure it's much good for retail as a whole when everyone just trains customers to wait for the sale. But we're doing it, so I'll be spending the next fortnight working down this list.
