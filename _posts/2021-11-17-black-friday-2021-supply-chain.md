Black Friday is a week on Friday, and most of the conversations I've had about it this year have been about whether the stock will be there at all.

The reasons are well reported by now. A shortage of HGV drivers that had petrol stations running dry in September. Container shipping costs several times what they were before the pandemic. Congestion at Felixstowe bad enough that shipping lines diverted vessels to other ports last month. Chip shortages holding up anything with electronics in it. Retailers have been telling customers to shop early for Christmas since the summer, which is a strange thing to hear from people whose business model usually depends on last-minute urgency.

## Why this one feels different

Last year the pandemic pushed Black Friday almost entirely online, and the main worry was whether websites and couriers could cope with the volume. This year the worry has moved further back down the chain. You can have a fast site, a well-built campaign and a healthy email list, and still not have the product to sell.

For luxury and heritage brands the picture is mixed. Goods made in Britain aren't sitting in a container off the coast, which is a real advantage right now. But raw materials, packaging and components often are imported, and the final mile still runs through the same stretched courier networks as everyone else.

I've written before that discounting sits awkwardly with luxury, and I haven't changed my mind. This year there's a practical argument on top of the brand one. If stock is tight, there's not much sense in discounting the things that would sell at full price anyway.

## Setting expectations

The thing I'd focus on this year is honesty with the customer. Some practical points:

1. **Show real stock levels.** If something is low, say so. If it's on back-order, say when you realistically expect it, and pad the estimate. A customer told "early January" who gets it on 28 December is happy. A customer told "before Christmas" who gets it on 28 December isn't.
2. **Put the Christmas cut-off date everywhere.** Product pages, basket, checkout, order confirmation, the top of every email. Work it out from your courier's published last dates, then add a day or two for your own handling.
3. **Promote what you actually have.** Build the Black Friday and Christmas emails around products with healthy stock, not around what was planned in August.
4. **Gift cards.** For a gift-heavy brand, a well-presented gift card or voucher is the honest answer to "I can't get it there in time". Make it easy to find and nice to receive.
5. **Brief customer service early.** The busiest emails in December will be "where is my order?". A clear holding reply, accurate tracking links and the authority to make small goodwill gestures will save hours.
6. **Check the returns window.** Plenty of retailers extend returns over Christmas so gifts can be exchanged in January. If you're doing that, say so clearly, and make sure whoever handles returns knows a wave is coming in the first fortnight of the new year.

## The risk of overpromising

The temptation in a year like this is to keep the marketing plan exactly as it was and hope operations catch up. That's how you end up with a strong week of sales followed by six weeks of apologies, refunds and one-star reviews.

A customer whose gift turns up late probably won't order again next year, and I'd take slightly lower sales in exchange for delivering what we promised.
