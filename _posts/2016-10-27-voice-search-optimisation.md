Amazon's Echo went on sale in the UK at the end of September. Google showed off Google Home at the beginning of this month, along with the Pixel phones and the Assistant built into them, and Home goes on sale in the US next week. Back in May, Sundar Pichai said that a fifth of queries on Google's mobile app and on Android devices are now voice searches.

So voice isn't a novelty any more, and "what's our voice search strategy?" is the sort of question that gets asked in every marketing team at the moment. My honest answer is that there's less to do than the hype suggests, but there are a few things, and they're mostly worth doing anyway.

## How voice queries are different

People talk to a device differently from how they type into a search box. A typed search might be "rolex submariner price". Spoken, it's more like "how much is a Rolex Submariner?" or "what's the difference between a Submariner and a Sea-Dweller?" Longer, more conversational, and much more often phrased as a question.

The other difference is the answer. On a screen, you get ten results and pick one. On a speaker, you get one answer read aloud, and that's it. There's no page two, and arguably no page one either; the assistant picks a source and reads it out.

## Where the answers come from

For Google, a lot of those spoken answers come from the same place as featured snippets: the box at the top of some results that pulls a direct answer from a web page and shows the source. When you ask Google a factual question by voice, the reply is often that snippet read out, with "according to" and the site name.

That's the most practical thing to know, because it means voice search optimisation, for now, is largely featured snippet optimisation. And featured snippets are something you can test and measure today.

Amazon is a different story. Alexa is largely answering from its own sources and from the "skills" that companies build for it, and searching the open web isn't really its strength. That's a bigger and more expensive question, and I'm not convinced most retailers need a skill this year.

## What I'd actually do

1. **Find the questions people ask.** Look at your search query data for anything phrased as a question, and use tools like Answer the Public or the "People also ask" boxes in Google to see what people want to know about your products and category.
2. **Answer them plainly, on a page.** A clear question as a heading, followed by a short, direct answer in the first paragraph, then the detail. Most sites bury the answer three paragraphs down, after some throat-clearing.
3. **Check which snippets you already own, or nearly own.** If you rank on page one for a question and someone else holds the snippet, look at how they've structured their answer. It's often just better formatted.
4. **Get the local basics right.** A lot of spoken searches are things like "watch shop near me" or "what time does it close". If your Google My Business listings are out of date, a voice assistant will happily read out the wrong opening hours.
5. **Keep an eye on structured data.** Marking up products, reviews and locations properly helps search engines understand the page, and it's sensible groundwork whatever happens with voice.

## What I wouldn't do

I wouldn't rebuild a content plan around voice, or start writing awkward pages stuffed with "near me" and "OK Google" phrasing. The measurement isn't there yet either. Google doesn't separate voice queries in Search Console or AdWords, so you can't really see what voice is doing for you, and I'm wary of spending real money on a channel I can't measure.

In luxury there's also an obvious ceiling. Nobody's going to buy an expensive watch by asking a speaker in the kitchen to order one. Voice is far more likely to be part of the research, someone asking a question about a model or a brand, and that's exactly where featured snippets come in.

The useful part of all this is that question-led, clearly answered content is good for ordinary search as well, so the work isn't wasted even if voice takes longer to matter than the headlines say.
