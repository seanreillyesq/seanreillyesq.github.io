I ran digital for a luxury hotel group, and the question I heard most was whether pushing guests to book direct was worth the effort. Commission is easy to see on a statement. The cost of winning a direct booking is spread across search, metasearch, the booking engine and the rate you give away, so most comparisons flatter one side or the other.

This puts both channels on the same footing: what you keep from one room night once every cost is taken out. Then it asks what moving a few points of your mix is worth over a year.

This model has two channels only: OTAs (Booking.com, Expedia and similar) and direct (your website, phone and email). Wholesale, corporate and group business are not modelled.

What each channel is worth

Net revenue is what you keep from a room night after the channel's own costs. It is before housekeeping, utilities, staff and every other cost of running the room, which are the same whichever channel the guest came through.

How this works

Everything is worked out per room night first, then scaled up to a year. ADR is what the guest pays, so commission and discounts are taken from that figure.

  • OTA net per night = ADR x (1 - commission)
  • Direct net per night = ADR x (1 - discount) x (1 - marketing - fees). The discount comes off the rate first, and marketing and fees are then taken as a share of the discounted rate the guest actually pays.
  • Room nights a year = rooms x 365 x occupancy
  • Net revenue by channel = room nights in that channel x net per night. The OTA share is the percentage you enter and the rest is direct.
  • Annual gain from the shift = room nights x (shift / 100) x (direct net - OTA net). If direct nets less than OTA, this is a loss, and the page says so.
  • One point is worth = the same calculation for a shift of one percentage point.
  • Break-even marketing is the direct marketing cost at which the two channels net the same, with every other input held where you set it.

The shift cannot be larger than the OTA share you start with, because you cannot move more room nights than you have there. Figures are calculated from the numbers you enter, not from rounded ones, and only the display is rounded.

What the model leaves out. It is a model of mix, not of demand, so treat the answer as a good guide to the order of magnitude rather than a forecast.

  • The billboard effect. OTA listings send people to your own website to book. Cut OTA exposure hard and you may lose some direct bookings too. The model assumes the direct room nights you win are extra to the ones you already have, and that nothing else falls.
  • Occupancy is fixed. Moving a booking from one channel to another does not change the number of rooms sold. If the shift costs you volume, the gain here is overstated.
  • Parity clauses and rate rules. Your contracts and local rules may limit how far below the OTA rate you can price direct, and how you can show member rates. The discount field is only useful if you are allowed to offer it.
  • Cancellations and no-shows. These often differ by channel, and a booking that cancels earns nothing. The model treats every booking as stayed.
  • Guest lifetime value and data. A direct guest can be emailed, rebooked and sold extras without paying commission again. That value is real but is not in these figures, so if anything the model understates direct.
  • Other commissioned business. Extras such as breakfast, spa and parking, and commission on those, are ignored. Marketing cost is one blended percentage, whereas the real cost of the next direct booking is usually higher than the average.
